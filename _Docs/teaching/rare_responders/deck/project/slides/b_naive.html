The lesson: wells are the unit of evidence, not nanowells (slide 29)

A natural question is why this model is needed at all. The table runs the three molecules through three simpler tests. Fisher’s exact test on pooled nanowells adds up each group’s nanowells, 96 of 4,000 for A and for B against 40 of 4,000 for control, and treats all 4,000 as independent. Because A and B have the same total it gives them the same p-value, about one in a million, so it calls B as strongly as A, although B’s total comes from a single well. A t-test on the eight counts uses one number per well and so is not fooled: it gives 0.0003 for A and 0.19 for B. A rank test agrees, but with four and four wells its p-value cannot go below 1 in 70. These well-level tests reach the same verdicts here. The model of this deck does what they do, one number per well, and adds what they lack: it knows the count is out of n nanowells with counting noise, it estimates the well-to-well scatter, it copes with wells of different sizes, and it reports an effect size. The lesson is that wells, not nanowells, are the unit of evidence.
