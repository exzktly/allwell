Confidence comes from replicate wells
How precisely a group’s rate is known (± % points, 1 SE, r = 0.01)

The question the test asks is whether a molecule’s wells sit further from the control wells than replicate wells of one condition differ from each other. So the confidence comes from the replicate wells, and this slide shows why. Nanowells in the same well share its conditions, so they partly repeat each other. Top left: at r = 0.01, a well of a thousand nanowells carries about as much evidence as 91 independent nanowells, a thousand divided by the multiplier of 11 from slide 10. Bottom left: one treated well with 20 exceedances is not convincing, since 15 % of untreated wells reach that by chance; three independent replicate wells that all reach it are, about 0.15 cubed, 0.34 %. On the right, both charts show the same thing on the same axis: how precisely a group’s rate is known, as one standard error in percentage points, for a group whose wells differ by r = 0.01. Left: keep four replicate wells and give each more nanowells. Going from 100 to 1,000 nanowells helps, but past that the precision stops improving at about plus or minus 0.5, because the wells’ own differences do not average away inside a well. Right: keep 1,000 nanowells per well and add replicate wells. The precision keeps improving, roughly with the square root of the number of wells. So a molecule is called different from the controls when its excess is large next to the spread between replicate wells, and the way to detect smaller effects is to add wells, not nanowells.
