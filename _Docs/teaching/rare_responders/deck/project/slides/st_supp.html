Two supporting tests are reported beside the verdict, which rests on the exceedance test. Test B, Higher Criticism, needs no cutoff. Each of the well’s nanowells gets a p-value against the pooled control nanowells: one plus the number of control nanowells at least as high, over the number of control nanowells plus one. The p-values are ranked, smallest first. If nothing is going on they spread evenly, the j-th near j over n, the dashed line. HC measures, at each rank, how far the observed p-value falls below that line in standard-error units, and takes the maximum over the most extreme 10 % of nanowells, skipping p-values at or below 1 over n, where one nanowell could dominate. The rank where it peaks, here 14, gives the candidate responders. Test C, the upper-tail Anderson–Darling statistic, compares the whole upper half of the well’s distribution with the controls’, counting only where the well has more mass high up, and weighting gaps far out in the tail most. It also rises when the whole distribution shifts, so on its own it signals a shape change, not rare responders. Neither statistic is read from a textbook table: their p-values come from shuffling whole wells. Every way of labelling c + t wells as t treated is tried, here C(8, 4) = 70, the statistic is averaged over the labelled wells, and p is the share of labellings at least as extreme as the real one. Its floor is one over the number of labellings, 1/70 here, and 0.1 with 3 control and 2 treated wells, which is why these tests need more wells than the exceedance test. With fewer than 3 control and 2 treated wells, the exceedance test falls back to a pooled Fisher exact test, labelled anti-conservative because it treats nanowells as independent.
