How it is done
1. Sort the p-values, smallest first: that order is the rank
2. A molecule needs p below 0.05 × rank ÷ 3
    The smallest p faces the strictest limit
3. Equivalently, q = p × 3 ÷ rank
    Each q is then lowered to the smallest q at its rank or above (no change here)
4. Called when q < 0.05
C: p = 0.041 is below 0.05 but above its limit, 0.033, so q = 0.061: not called

Assumes the molecules’ tests are independent or positively related
Sharing the same control wells keeps them positively related
The correction runs within each donor: over that donor’s molecules only

With several molecules on a plate, each tested at p below 0.05, the chance of at least one false call grows with the number of molecules: about 14 % for three that do nothing. The Benjamini–Hochberg procedure corrects for this. Sort the p-values from smallest to largest, compare the one at rank k with 0.05 times k divided by the number of molecules, and call significant every molecule up to the largest rank that passes. Each molecule gets a corrected value, q, which is never smaller than its p, and the molecule is called significant when q is below 0.05. Here molecule A passes easily. Molecule C, whose four wells are all a little above the controls, has p = 0.041, which would count on its own, but it is compared with 0.033 at rank two and fails: its q is 0.061. Molecule B is not close. The procedure keeps the expected share of false calls among the molecules called significant at 5 %. Counts and p-values are illustrative.
