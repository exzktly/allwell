A fair question about the exceedance test: what if a treatment does not create rare responders but lifts every nanowell a little, for example through a cytokine or medium effect that reaches every nanowell? The cutoff is fixed by the control wells, at their 99th percentile. If the whole treated distribution moves up, more nanowells cross that fixed line even though none of them responds strongly. Here a made-up shift of 1.2-fold, with the shape unchanged, takes the count from about 10 to 35 per well, more than molecule A’s rare responders in the middle, and the exceedance test would call it detected. So the exceedance test alone cannot distinguish rare responders from a bulk shift. The two are told apart by the bulk: in the rare-responder case the median nanowell of each well is unchanged, in the shift case it moves, and a test on the wells’ medians, the bulk question of slide 8, detects the shift while it would miss rare responders. The upper-tail shape test reported beside the verdict also flags a shifted tail; on its own, a shape-test signal is a shift, not rare responders. Normalising each well by its own bulk would remove such a shift, but it would equally remove a real treatment effect on the bulk, so it is off by default. In practice: when the verdict is detected, look at the treated wells’ medians before calling the effect rare responders. A second look-alike is seeding: nanowells that started with fewer cells reach extreme fold changes more easily, so if the treatment wells were seeded differently from the controls, that alone can look like rare responders. The verdict is left as it is; compare the wells’ starting-cell distributions before trusting it.
