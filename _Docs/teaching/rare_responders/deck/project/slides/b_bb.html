2. The beta-binomial: which counts Kᵢ follow

x-axis: Kᵢ, height: the chance P of that count

dashed: r = 0, the binomial
navy and green: the two Betas on the left

K = 64

πᵢ ~ Beta(a, b), with a = π(1 − r) ÷ r and b = (1 − π)(1 − r) ÷ r
Step 4 for A: a = 6.4, b = 367
P(Kᵢ | π, r) = C(n, Kᵢ) × B(Kᵢ + a, n − Kᵢ + b) ÷ B(a, b)
B(·, ·): the beta function, which does the averaging over πᵢ
As r → 0 the Beta narrows to a spike at π, and P becomes the binomial

The model’s two stages can be drawn. The left panel is the first stage: the Beta distribution says which rates a single well may have. For step 4 of A’s test, with a mean of 1.7 percent and r = 0.0027, wells’ rates mostly lie between about 1 and 2.6 percent. For B’s fit, with r = 0.008, the curve is much wider and skewed to the right: the typical well has a rate near 1.8 percent, and 95 percent of wells lie between about 0.4 and 5.2 percent. The right panel is the second stage, after counting: the chance of each count K. With r = 0 it is the binomial, dashed. With r = 0.0027 it is wider. With B’s r it has a long right tail, which is how the model accommodates a well at 64 without believing the whole group is high. The formulas: the Beta has two shape numbers, a and b, set by the mean and r; for step 4 they are about 6.4 and 367. The chance of a count is the binomial averaged over every rate the Beta allows, which works out to a ratio of beta functions. As r goes to zero the Beta narrows to a spike at the mean and the formula becomes the plain binomial.
