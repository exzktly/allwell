This slide collects the whole algorithm on one page, with molecule A’s numbers. Step 1: the cutoff is the 99th percentile of the control nanowells’ fold change, 2.2-fold here. Step 2: for each well, count the nanowells above the cutoff; that count K is all the test uses. Step 3: fit the model with a gap, by finding the α, δ and r that give the eight counts the highest log-likelihood; for A that is −20.2, with δ = 0.89. Step 4: fit again with δ held at zero, one rate for all eight wells; r is refitted and rises to 0.0027, and the log-likelihood drops to −27.4. Step 5: twice the difference is the likelihood ratio, 14.24; its square root is z, 3.77, and p is the area of a standard normal curve beyond z, 0.00008. Step 6: because several molecules were tested, each p is corrected by Benjamini–Hochberg into q; A’s q is 0.00024, below 0.05, so A is called a source of rare responders. Every molecule goes through steps 3 to 5 against the same control wells, and step 6 is applied once to all their p-values together. The last column runs molecule B through the same six steps. Its wells average 24, like A’s, but one well at 64 makes the fit set r to 0.008; the two fits then differ by only 0.4 in log L, D is 0.89, z 0.95 and p 0.17, and after correction q is 0.17, so B is not called.
