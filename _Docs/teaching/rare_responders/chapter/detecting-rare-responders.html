Chapter

Detecting Rare Responders

How to test whether a treatment produces a few fast-growing nanowells per well, when the replicate is the well

Some treatments do not change what a typical cell does. They change what a few cells do. A drug might leave 99 % of a population growing as before while a handful of cells grow many times faster. Finding such an effect is a statistical problem of its own, because the usual methods are built to detect changes in the typical cell and are blind to changes in a few.

This chapter develops the statistics of that problem from the beginning, using one kind of experiment as its running example: a plate of wells, each well holding about a thousand small chambers of cells. It assumes a first course in statistics: means and variances, percentiles, the normal curve, and what a p-value is. The tools beyond that, from the binomial distribution to likelihood, are set out in section 2 before they are used.

After this chapter you should be able to
  • explain why a test of the average cannot detect a rare effect, and why counting in the upper tail can;
  • separate the two sources of random variation in a count, counting noise and differences between wells, and say what the intra-well correlation r measures;
  • explain why the well, not the nanowell, is the unit of replication, and what goes wrong when that is ignored;
  • follow the exceedance test step by step, from a model of one well's count to a p-value and an odds ratio;
  • describe what Higher Criticism and the shape test add, and why a test built by relabelling wells has a smallest possible p-value;
  • apply the Benjamini–Hochberg correction when several treatments are tested;
  • state how many control and treated wells an experiment needs for a given goal, and why extra nanowells cannot stand in for extra wells.

1. The question, and why only the upper tail can answer it

Does a treatment make a few nanowells grow far faster than untreated nanowells normally do?

The experiment. Cells are grown in a plate: a flat plastic tray moulded with a grid of separate compartments called wells, commonly 96 of them. Each well is filled with nutrient liquid (the medium) and receives one treatment, such as a drug at one dose, or no treatment at all. Wells that receive no treatment are control wells; they show what untreated cells do. The floor of each well is itself divided into about 1,000 tiny chambers called nanowells. At the start, a few cells are placed in each nanowell (this is called seeding). The cells then grow for some days, and a microscope photographs every nanowell at the start and at the end so that its cells can be counted.

Each nanowell is summarised by one number, its fold change: FC = cells at the end ÷ cells at the start. A nanowell that went from 2 cells to 8 has FC 4. A responder is a nanowell in which the treatment caused far more growth than untreated nanowells show. The effect of interest is rare: perhaps 5 to 20 responders in a well of 1,000.

An effect that rare is invisible to any test of the average. Suppose untreated nanowells average FC 1 and a treatment turns 10 of 1,000 into responders at FC 4. The mean rises by 3 % (1.00 → 1.03). The cumulative curve, the share of nanowells at or below each FC, moves by at most 0.01. The familiar tests compare two sets of values through their means (the t-test), through the order of their values (the Mann–Whitney test), or through the largest distance between their cumulative curves (the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test). All three are driven by the bulk, the typical nanowells, and the bulk has not changed.

Figure 1 draws this case. Panel A shows the FC distribution of a whole untreated well and of a whole treated well: the two curves cannot be told apart. Panel B magnifies the far right of the same two curves. There the treated well holds about twice as many nanowells as the untreated one.

The method therefore ignores the bulk on purpose and examines only the upper tail: the few highest-FC nanowells, where the extreme events are. It needs a line that says where the tail starts and a count of what lies beyond it.

  • Cutoff. The 99th percentile of FC among all control nanowells, pooled across the control wells. Control wells are wells that receive no treatment; they show what untreated nanowells do. By construction, 1 % of control nanowells lie above it.
  • Exceedance. A nanowell whose FC is above the cutoff. Exceedances are counted within one well, out of that well's nanowells. In Figure 1B the untreated well has 10 exceedances among its 1,000 nanowells and the treated well has 20.

A responder and an exceedance are not the same thing. A responder is a cause: a nanowell that the treatment made grow fast. An exceedance is an observation: a nanowell whose FC came out above the cutoff, for whatever reason. An untreated well has no responders at all and still has about 10 exceedances, because 1 % of ordinary nanowells grow that much by themselves. In the treated well of Figure 1B, 10 of the 20 exceedances are responders and 10 are ordinary, and nothing marks which are which. Responders cannot be picked out one by one. All that can be done is to count exceedances and ask whether there are more than ordinary growth explains.

The whole problem is then one question: is 20 more than an untreated well would show by chance? Section 2 sets out the mathematical tools needed. Section 3 answers how much untreated wells vary. Section 4 describes the tests built on that answer.

One rule runs through everything below. The treatment is applied to a well, not to a nanowell, so the replicate is the well. A thousand nanowells in one well are a thousand measurements of that one well, not a thousand independent tests of the treatment.

2. The tools this chapter uses

The chapter assumes a first course in statistics: probability, the mean, variance and standard deviation of a random variable, percentiles and cumulative curves, the normal curve, and the logic of a hypothesis test and its p-value. This section adds the tools that go beyond that and that the later sections depend on. Nothing here is specific to nanowells. Later sections point back here by number whenever a tool is used.

2.1 Yes-or-no outcomes and the binomial distribution

Many quantities in this chapter are counts of how many items, out of a fixed number, have some property. The building block is a single yes-or-no outcome.

One yes-or-no outcome. Let X be 1 with probability π and 0 with probability 1 − π. (Here π is simply a symbol for a probability; it is not the number 3.14.) Its mean, written E[X] (the E stands for "expected value"), is 1 × π + 0 × (1 − π) = π. Its two distances from the mean are 1 − π (with probability π) and −π (with probability 1 − π), so its variance is (1 − π) × (1 − π) × π + π × π × (1 − π). Both terms contain π × (1 − π); taking that out leaves π(1 − π) × [(1 − π) + π], and the bracket is 1. So:

\[\operatorname{E}[X] \;=\; \pi, \qquad \operatorname{Var}[X] \;=\; \pi(1-\pi) \tag{1}\]

A count of n independent yes-or-no outcomes. Let K be the number of "yes" results among n independent outcomes that each have probability π. K is the sum of n of the variables above. Means always add, and the variances of independent variables add, so its mean is n × π and its variance is n × π(1 − π). The distribution of K is called the binomial distribution. Its probabilities are:

\[P(K = k) \;=\; C(n,k)\;\pi^{k}\,(1-\pi)^{\,n-k}, \qquad \operatorname{E}[K] = n\pi, \qquad \operatorname{Var}[K] = n\pi(1-\pi) \tag{2}\]

In words: πk(1 − π)n − k is the probability of one particular arrangement of k "yes" and n − k "no" results, and C(n, k) is the number of different arrangements that give k "yes" results (section 2.5). For 10 tosses of a fair coin (n = 10, π = 0.5), the mean is 10 × 0.5 = 5 heads and the variance is 10 × 0.5 × 0.5 = 2.5, an SD of 1.58.

Worked example: a rare property

Suppose 1,000 independent items each have a 1 % probability of some property. The count with the property has mean 1,000 × 0.01 = 10 and variance 1,000 × 0.01 × 0.99 = 9.9, an SD of √9.9 = 3.1. When π is small, 1 − π is close to 1, so the variance of a count of rare events is close to its mean. Repeating the whole count would give something between about 7 and 13 (the mean plus or minus one SD) roughly three times in four, and now and then a value outside that range.

This scatter, which is present even when the probability π is fixed and known exactly, is called counting noise in this chapter.

2.2 Covariance and correlation

Two random variables can move together. The covariance measures that: it is the average of the product of their two distances from their own means. In the formula, μX and μY are the means of X and Y.

\[\operatorname{Cov}[X,Y] \;=\; \operatorname{E}\bigl[(X-\mu_X)(Y-\mu_Y)\bigr] \tag{3}\]

When X tends to be above its mean at the same time as Y is above its mean, the products are mostly positive and the covariance is positive. When the two are independent, the products cancel on average and the covariance is 0.

A covariance depends on the units of X and Y, which makes it hard to read. Dividing it by the two SDs removes the units and gives the correlation of X and Y, written Corr[X, Y], a number between −1 and +1:

\[\operatorname{Corr}[X,Y] \;=\; \frac{\operatorname{Cov}[X,Y]}{\mathrm{SD}[X]\;\mathrm{SD}[Y]} \tag{4}\]

A correlation of 0 means no linear relationship; +1 means the two move in perfect step. Rearranged, equation (4) says Cov[X, Y] = Corr[X, Y] × SD[X] × SD[Y]. For two variables with the same variance, the covariance is their correlation times that common variance.

The variance of a sum, in general. When the variables added together are not independent, their variances no longer simply add. For two variables:

\[\operatorname{Var}[X+Y] \;=\; \operatorname{Var}[X] + \operatorname{Var}[Y] + 2\operatorname{Cov}[X,Y] \tag{5}\]

The covariance is counted twice, once for the pair (X, Y) and once for the pair (Y, X). For a sum of many variables the pattern is the same: add every variable's variance, then add the covariance of every ordered pair of different variables. With n variables there are n variances and n × (n − 1) ordered pairs. Positive covariances therefore make a sum more variable than independence would predict, and with many variables the number of pairs is much larger than the number of variables. Sections 2.3 and 3.2 use exactly this.

2.3 The beta distribution and the beta-binomial distribution

The binomial distribution of section 2.1 assumes that every one of the n outcomes has the same probability and that the outcomes are independent. In many experiments the items being counted come in groups, and the probability differs from group to group. This section works out what that does to a count. It is the longest section of the chapter because the main test of section 4.1 is built on it.

Where this section goes. One example is used throughout: a bakery. The section has six parts.

  1. The setting. A count that comes about in two stages, and the two reasons it varies.
  2. The number r. One number, between 0 and 1, for how much the groups differ.
  3. The same r is a correlation. Items of the same group are correlated exactly as much as groups differ.
  4. The variance of the count. Where the factor 1 + (n − 1)r comes from.
  5. What a group is worth as evidence. The effective number of items.
  6. The beta and beta-binomial distributions. The specific curves needed to turn all this into probabilities.

Parts 1 to 5 need no particular curve; they hold for any way the groups might differ. The beta distribution named in the title enters only in Part 6.

The example, fixed for the whole section. A bakery bakes biscuits on trays of 50. Across everything it bakes, 20 % of biscuits come out burnt. In the general language of this section a tray is a group, a biscuit is an item, and "burnt" is the "yes" outcome. From section 3 on, the group will be a well, the item a nanowell, and "yes" will mean "exceeds a cutoff"; nothing in this section depends on that. Three numbers describe the bakery and do not change:

  • n = 50, the number of biscuits on a tray;
  • π = 0.2, the bakery's overall burn probability (Part 1 says exactly what this means);
  • r = 0.1, the number that says how much its trays differ (Part 2 defines it).

This is called the main bakery below. Three times the section steps away from it to a simpler or more extreme case. Each time the text says so before it does, and says when it returns.

Part 1. The setting: a count made in two stages

Whether a particular biscuit burns depends on two things. One is its tray: some trays sit on a hotter shelf, go in a minute late, or are spread a little thinner, and that affects every biscuit on the tray at once. The other is the luck of that one biscuit: its exact thickness, where the heat happened to catch it. So the number of burnt biscuits on a tray comes about in two stages, drawn in Figure 2.

  • Stage 1: the tray gets its own burn probability, written p. Before any biscuit is looked at, the tray's p is already settled by its shelf, its timing and how it was spread. It differs from tray to tray. The left panel of Figure 2 shows how the values of p are spread across the main bakery's trays, with four example trays marked A to D.
  • Stage 2: each biscuit on that tray burns, or not, with that tray's p. The right panel of Figure 2 shows the same four trays as they came out of the oven. Tray A, with p = 0.06, burnt 2 of its 50 biscuits. Tray D, with p = 0.42, burnt 23.

K is the number of burnt biscuits on one tray. It is the count this whole section is about.

Two probabilities, at two levels. The example has two different burn probabilities, and they must be kept apart.

  • p belongs to one tray. It is the chance that a biscuit on that tray burns. Tray A has p = 0.06 and tray D has p = 0.42.
  • π belongs to the bakery. It is the average of p over all the trays, a single number, here 0.2. It is not the burn probability of any particular tray, and no tray need have exactly that value.

The two answer different questions. Pick a biscuit knowing which tray it came from: the chance it is burnt is that tray's p. Pick a biscuit at random from everything the bakery baked, without knowing its tray: the chance it is burnt is π. In section 2.1 the letter π stood for the one probability shared by every outcome. Here the outcomes do not share one probability, and π is the average of the trays' probabilities. When every tray has the same p, the two meanings coincide.

Two meanings of "independent". The same care is needed with this word.

  • Within a tray whose p is known, the biscuits are independent: once the tray's p is fixed, whether one biscuit burns says nothing more about another. That is stage 2.
  • When the tray's p is not known, two biscuits from the same tray are not independent. A burnt biscuit is a sign that its tray has a high p, and a high p makes the tray's other biscuits more likely to be burnt too. Part 3 measures exactly how much.

Different trays are independent of one another in every sense: each tray gets its p separately, and a hot tray says nothing about the next tray.

What "the variance of K" is taken over. It is taken over trays. Picture a long row of trays from the main bakery. Count the burnt biscuits on each and write the counts down: 2, 9, 11, 23, and so on, one number per tray. The mean of K is the average of that list, and the variance of K is the average squared distance of the counts from that mean. It is not a variation among the biscuits within one tray; one tray has only one count.

Two reasons the counts in that list differ. They are given names here, and the same two names are used for the rest of the chapter.

  • Counting noise. Even if every tray had exactly the same p, the counts would not all be equal, because which biscuits burn is luck. Counting noise is defined as the variance K would have in that case, with every tray at p = π. That is the binomial variance of section 2.1: nπ(1 − π) = 50 × 0.2 × 0.8 = 8.
  • Tray differences. The trays do not all have the same p, so the counts differ by more than that. Tray differences is defined as everything the variance of K has beyond the counting noise. Its size is not yet known; finding it is the work of Parts 2 to 4.

Part 2. The number r: how much the trays differ

The trays' probabilities p form a list too, one value per tray. Its mean is π. Its variance, written Var[p], is the average squared distance of the trays' values of p from π. Var[p] is 0 when all trays are alike and grows as they differ more. The number r is that variance measured against π(1 − π):

\[r \;=\; \frac{\operatorname{Var}[p]}{\pi(1-\pi)} \tag{6}\]

The reason for dividing by π(1 − π) is that π(1 − π) is the largest value Var[p] can possibly have, so r is the variance of the trays' probabilities as a fraction of its largest possible value. That makes r a number between 0 and 1. The next three paragraphs show this; they are also the first two of the three departures from the main bakery.

Departure 1, the lowest value: a bakery with r = 0. Every tray has p = 0.2 exactly. No tray is any distance from the mean, so Var[p] = 0 and r = 0. A variance cannot be negative, so r cannot go below this. In this bakery K follows the binomial distribution of section 2.1 and its variance is the counting noise alone, 8. Learning that one biscuit burnt tells nothing about its neighbour, because all trays are alike.

Departure 2, the highest value: a bakery with r = 1. One tray in five sits in a part of the oven that burns everything (p = 1) and the other four in five burn nothing (p = 0). The average is still π = 0.2. A tray with p = 1 is at distance 1 − 0.2 = 0.8 from the mean, and a tray with p = 0 is at distance 0 − 0.2 = −0.2. So Var[p] = 0.2 × (0.8 × 0.8) + 0.8 × (0.2 × 0.2) = 0.128 + 0.032 = 0.16, which is π(1 − π) = 0.2 × 0.8 = 0.16, and r = 0.16 / 0.16 = 1. In this bakery every tray comes out with K = 0 or K = 50 and nothing between. Learning that one biscuit burnt tells that every other biscuit on its tray burnt too.

Why nothing can exceed that. Multiply out the definition of the variance. Var[p] is the average of (p − π) × (p − π), and (p − π) × (p − π) = p × p − 2 × π × p + π × π. Average each term, using that the average of p is π: the result is E[p × p] − 2 × π × π + π × π. So

Var[p] = E[p × p] − π × π.

This fact is used again in Part 3. Here it gives the limit. A probability p lies between 0 and 1, and for such a number p × p is never larger than p (0.3 × 0.3 = 0.09 is less than 0.3). So E[p × p] is never larger than E[p], which is π. Therefore Var[p] is never larger than π − π × π = π(1 − π). It equals π(1 − π) only when p × p = p on every tray, which happens only when every p is 0 or 1: the all-or-nothing bakery.

Back to the main bakery. It has r = 0.1: its trays differ by a tenth of the most they could. By equation (6), Var[p] = r × π(1 − π) = 0.1 × 0.16 = 0.016. The SD of p is the square root, 0.13. So a tray one SD below the average has p = 0.2 − 0.13 = 0.07 and a tray one SD above has p = 0.2 + 0.13 = 0.33. That is the spread drawn in the left panel of Figure 2. A small r means trays are nearly alike; a large r means the tray matters more than the luck of the biscuit.

From here to the end of the chapter, r always means the quantity defined by equation (6).

Part 3. The same r is the correlation between two biscuits on a tray

The two extreme bakeries already suggest a link. When trays were identical, a burnt biscuit said nothing about its neighbour. When trays differed as much as possible, it said everything. This part shows that the link is exact: the correlation between two biscuits on the same tray, in the sense of section 2.2, is the number r of equation (6). It is done first with numbers, in Boxes 3a and 3b, and then in general, in Box 3c.

Departure 3: a two-shelf bakery. For the numbers to be easy to follow, Boxes 3a and 3b use a bakery with only two kinds of tray. Half its trays are on a cool shelf with p = 0.1 and half on a hot shelf with p = 0.3. Its average is still π = 0.2. Its r turns out to be 0.0625, as Box 3b shows. The main bakery returns in Part 4.

Pick one tray of the two-shelf bakery at random, without knowing its shelf, and look at two biscuits on that tray. Call them the first biscuit and the second biscuit. Write X for the first biscuit's outcome, X = 1 if it burnt and X = 0 if not, and Y for the second biscuit's outcome in the same way. "Both" below always means these two biscuits.

Box 3a. The covariance between the two biscuits, in the two-shelf bakery
  1. The mean of X. The first biscuit burns with probability 0.1 on a cool-shelf tray and 0.3 on a hot-shelf tray. Half the trays are of each kind, so overall it burns with probability 0.5 × 0.1 + 0.5 × 0.3 = 0.2. The mean of a yes-or-no outcome is its probability of being 1 (equation (1)), so the mean of X is 0.2.
  2. The mean of Y. The second biscuit is on the same tray, so the same calculation gives 0.2.
  3. The definition. Equation (3): Cov[X, Y] is the average of (X − 0.2) × (Y − 0.2). For each possible outcome of the pair, multiply the first biscuit's distance from its mean by the second biscuit's distance from its mean; then average those products, weighting each by its probability.
  4. The two possible distances. A burnt biscuit has outcome 1, so its distance from the mean is 1 − 0.2 = +0.8. An unburnt biscuit has outcome 0, so its distance is 0 − 0.2 = −0.2.
  5. The four possible outcomes for the pair. Both burnt; first burnt and second not; second burnt and first not; neither burnt.
  6. Probability that both burnt. On a cool-shelf tray each biscuit burns with probability 0.1, and with the tray's p fixed the two are independent, so both burn with probability 0.1 × 0.1 = 0.01. On a hot-shelf tray: 0.3 × 0.3 = 0.09. Half the trays are of each kind: 0.5 × 0.01 + 0.5 × 0.09 = 0.05.
  7. Probability of "first burnt, second not". The first biscuit burns with probability 0.2 in total (line 1). That total is made of two cases: the second also burnt (0.05, line 6), or the second did not. So this outcome has probability 0.2 − 0.05 = 0.15.
  8. Probability of "second burnt, first not". The same argument with the two biscuits exchanged: 0.2 − 0.05 = 0.15.
  9. Probability that neither burnt. The four probabilities add to 1, so it is 1 − 0.05 − 0.15 − 0.15 = 0.65.
  10. The product of distances for each outcome, using line 4.
    Outcome for the pairProbability(X − 0.2) × (Y − 0.2)Probability × product

  12. Result. Cov[X, Y] = 0.01. It is positive: the two biscuits tend to burn together.

What the positive covariance means can be seen directly. If the two biscuits were independent, both would burn with probability 0.2 × 0.2 = 0.04. The actual figure, from line 6, is 0.05. Put another way: among trays whose first biscuit burnt, the second biscuit burnt with probability 0.05 / 0.2 = 0.25, not the overall 0.2, because a burnt first biscuit makes it more likely that the tray is on the hot shelf.

Box 3b. The variance of the trays' burn probabilities, in the two-shelf bakery
  1. The quantity. p is one tray's burn probability. Half the trays have p = 0.1 and half have p = 0.3. No biscuits are involved.
  2. The mean of p. 0.5 × 0.1 + 0.5 × 0.3 = 0.2. This is π.
  3. Squared distance for a cool-shelf tray. (0.1 − 0.2) × (0.1 − 0.2) = (−0.1) × (−0.1) = 0.01.
  4. Squared distance for a hot-shelf tray. (0.3 − 0.2) × (0.3 − 0.2) = 0.1 × 0.1 = 0.01.
  5. Average over the two kinds of tray. Var[p] = 0.5 × 0.01 + 0.5 × 0.01 = 0.01.
  6. The r of this bakery. By equation (6), r = Var[p] / (π(1 − π)) = 0.01 / (0.2 × 0.8) = 0.01 / 0.16 = 0.0625.

The two boxes reach the same number by unrelated routes. Box 3a looked at two biscuits and found a covariance of 0.01. Box 3b looked only at trays and found a variance of 0.01. This is not an accident of the numbers chosen. Box 3c repeats the reasoning with letters in place of numbers, so that it holds for any bakery: p is one tray's burn probability, whatever values it takes across trays, and π is the average of p.

Box 3c. Why the two are equal in every bakery
  1. Both biscuits burnt, on one tray. On a tray with burn probability p, each biscuit burns with probability p, and with p fixed the two are independent, so both burn with probability p × p. (Two-shelf bakery: 0.01 and 0.09.)
  2. Both biscuits burnt, over all trays. The overall probability is the average of p × p over the trays: P(both burnt) = E[p × p]. (Two-shelf bakery: 0.05.)
  3. Use the fact from Part 2. Var[p] = E[p × p] − π × π, so E[p × p] = π × π + Var[p]. With line 2: P(both burnt) = π × π + Var[p]. (Two-shelf bakery: 0.05 = 0.04 + 0.01.)
  4. The covariance, multiplied out. X and Y each have mean π. Cov[X, Y] is the average of (X − π) × (Y − π), and (X − π) × (Y − π) = X × Y − π × X − π × Y + π × π. Average each term, using that X and Y each average to π: the result is E[X × Y] − π × π − π × π + π × π = E[X × Y] − π × π.
  5. What E[X × Y] is. X and Y are each 0 or 1, so the product X × Y is 1 when both biscuits burnt and 0 otherwise. Its average is therefore the probability that both burnt. So Cov[X, Y] = P(both burnt) − π × π. In words: the covariance is the probability that both burnt, minus what that probability would be if the two biscuits were independent. (Two-shelf bakery: 0.05 − 0.04 = 0.01, agreeing with Box 3a.)
  6. Put line 3 into line 5. Cov[X, Y] = (π × π + Var[p]) − π × π = Var[p]. The π × π cancels. The covariance between two biscuits on a tray is the variance of the trays' burn probabilities, in every bakery.
  7. Bring in r. By equation (6), Var[p] = r × π(1 − π). So Cov[X, Y] = r × π(1 − π).
  8. From covariance to correlation. Equation (4) divides a covariance by the two SDs. X and Y have the same SD, so the product of the two SDs is the variance of one biscuit's outcome, which is π(1 − π) by equation (1). Dividing: Corr[X, Y] = r × π(1 − π) / (π(1 − π)) = r. (Two-shelf bakery: 0.01 / 0.16 = 0.0625, the r of Box 3b.)

Equation (7) collects lines 2, 3, 6 and 7, and line 8 is the sentence after it.

\[\begin{gathered} P(\text{both burnt}) \;=\; \operatorname{E}[\,p \times p\,] \;=\; \pi^2 + \operatorname{Var}[p] \\[8pt] \text{so}\qquad \operatorname{Cov}[X,Y] \;=\; \operatorname{Var}[p] \;=\; r\,\pi(1-\pi) \end{gathered} \tag{7}\]

The correlation between two biscuits on the same tray is r. This is why the quantity is given the letter usually used for a correlation.

r has two readings, and they are one number. Looking across trays, r is how much the trays' burn probabilities differ, as a fraction of the most they could (equation (6)). Looking inside one tray, r is the correlation between two of its biscuits (Box 3c). Biscuits on a tray are correlated for one reason only: they share a tray, and trays differ. If trays did not differ there would be nothing to share and the correlation would be zero. r says nothing about one tray resembling another; different trays are independent.

Part 4. The variance of the count: where 1 + (n − 1)r comes from

This part returns to the main bakery and finds the size of the tray differences that Part 1 left open.

K written as a sum. K is the number of burnt biscuits on one tray of n. Give each of the tray's n biscuits its own yes-or-no outcome, 1 if that biscuit burnt and 0 if not, as X and Y were for two biscuits in Part 3. Adding the n outcomes counts the 1s, and that count is K. For a tray of 5 biscuits with outcomes 1, 0, 0, 1, 0, the sum is 2 and K = 2 burnt biscuits. Writing K as a sum matters because section 2.2 gave a rule for the variance of a sum: add each item's own variance, then add the covariance of every ordered pair of different items.

Figure 3 lays the rule out as a table with one row and one column per biscuit. Each cell is one contribution to the variance of K. It is drawn for a tray of only 8 biscuits because a 50 by 50 table would be too fine to read; the counting is the same for any n. The figure's labels use the general words: an item is a biscuit and a group is a tray.

  • The n diagonal cells pair a biscuit with itself. Each contributes that biscuit's own variance, π(1 − π). Together: n × π(1 − π).
  • The n × (n − 1) other cells pair two different biscuits of the same tray. By equation (7) each contributes r × π(1 − π). Together: n × (n − 1) × r × π(1 − π).

Adding the two totals gives the variance of K:

\[\begin{gathered} \operatorname{Var}[K] \;=\; \underbrace{n\,\pi(1-\pi)}_{\substack{\text{counting noise:}\\ n \text{ diagonal cells}}} \;+\; \class{eqb}{\underbrace{n(n-1)\;r\,\pi(1-\pi)}_{\substack{\text{tray differences:}\\ n(n-1) \text{ other cells}}}} \\[10pt] \;=\; n\,\pi(1-\pi)\,\bigl[\,1+(n-1)\,r\,\bigr] \end{gathered} \tag{8}\]

The first term is the counting noise of Part 1: it is what the variance would be with r = 0, every tray alike. The second term is therefore the tray differences, whose size was the open question. The last line only collects the common factor nπ(1 − π). Inside its bracket, the "1" stands for the counting noise and "(n − 1)r" for the tray differences, measured in units of the counting noise. A count whose variance is larger than the binomial's is called overdispersed.

  4. Variance of K. 8 + 39.2 = 47.2. The same from the last line of equation (8): 8 × [1 + 49 × 0.1] = 8 × 5.9 = 47.2.
  5. SD of K. The square root of 47.2 is 6.9 biscuits. If all trays were alike it would be the square root of 8, which is 2.8.

Part 5. What a tray is worth as evidence: the effective number of items

Suppose the aim is to learn the bakery's overall burn probability π, and one tray of n biscuits has been examined. The share of its biscuits that burnt, K / n, is an estimate of π. How good an estimate? The answer is found by comparing it with an estimate made from biscuits that are truly independent of one another.

Box 5. How many independent biscuits one tray is worth
  1. The variance of a share. Dividing every count in a list by n divides every distance from the mean by n, and so divides every squared distance by n × n. So the variance of K / n is the variance of K divided by n × n.
  2. The share burnt on one tray. By line 1 and equation (8), its variance is nπ(1 − π)[1 + (n − 1)r] / (n × n) = π(1 − π)[1 + (n − 1)r] / n.
  3. The comparison. Instead take m biscuits, one each from m different trays. Different trays are independent, so these m biscuits are independent, and each burns with probability π. Their count is binomial with variance mπ(1 − π) (section 2.1), so by line 1 the share burnt among them has variance mπ(1 − π) / (m × m) = π(1 − π) / m.
  4. Set the two variances equal. The tray and the m independent biscuits are equally good estimates of π when π(1 − π) / m = π(1 − π)[1 + (n − 1)r] / n.
  5. Solve for m. Divide both sides by π(1 − π): 1 / m = [1 + (n − 1)r] / n. Turn both sides upside down: m = n / [1 + (n − 1)r].

That value of m is called the effective number of items and written neff:

\[n_{\text{eff}} \;=\; \frac{n}{1+(n-1)\,r} \tag{9}\]

It is n divided by the same bracket that inflates the variance in equation (8). Three cases:

  • r = 0 (trays alike): neff = n / 1 = n. Every biscuit counts in full.
  • r = 1 (all or nothing): neff = n / [1 + (n − 1)] = n / n = 1. The whole tray is worth one biscuit, as Part 2 found.
  • The main bakery, r = 0.1: the bracket is 1 + 49 × 0.1 = 5.9, so neff = 50 / 5.9 = 8.5. A tray of 50 biscuits tells as much about π as 8.5 independent biscuits.

A larger tray does not help much. Keep r = 0.1 and make the tray bigger. With n = 500 the bracket is 1 + 499 × 0.1 = 50.9 and neff = 500 / 50.9 = 9.8. With n = 5,000 the bracket is 500.9 and neff = 5,000 / 500.9 = 9.98. The effective number creeps toward 10 and never reaches it. The reason is in the bracket: for large n, the 1 is negligible beside (n − 1)r, and (n − 1)r is nearly n × r, so neff is nearly n / (n × r) = 1 / r. Here 1 / r = 1 / 0.1 = 10. Figure 4 plots equation (9) for four values of r. Each curve levels off at its own 1 / r.

The main point of this section. Examining more biscuits on one tray pins down that one tray's own p ever more precisely. It does not pin down π, the average over trays, because one tray's p is a single draw from the spread of trays, however exactly it is known. The effective number of items measures information about π, and that is why it stops growing. The only way to learn about π, or about a difference between two kinds of tray, is to examine more trays. The group, not the item, is the unit of evidence.

Part 6. The beta and beta-binomial distributions: from variances to probabilities

Everything in Parts 2 to 5 used only the mean π and the variance of the trays' probabilities. None of it needed to know the shape of the spread of p: equations (6) to (9) hold for the two-shelf bakery, the all-or-nothing bakery and the main bakery alike. A shape is needed for one further thing: the probability of a particular count, such as "what is the chance that a tray has 20 or more burnt biscuits?" A variance cannot answer that. A full distribution can.

The beta distribution: a curve for the trays' probabilities. A tray's p must lie between 0 and 1. The standard family of curves for a quantity confined to that range is the beta distribution. It has two positive parameters, a and b, and its density (the curve whose area over any interval is the share of trays whose p falls in that interval) is

\[f(p) \;=\; \frac{p^{\,a-1}\,(1-p)^{\,b-1}}{B(a,b)}, \qquad 0 \le p \le 1 \tag{10}\]

B(a, b) is the beta function: a constant, depending only on a and b, whose job is to make the total area under the curve equal to 1. Software computes it. Different choices of a and b give curves that are humped, skewed toward 0, skewed toward 1, or nearly flat, which is why the family is used. The formula is shown so that the family has a definite meaning; nothing below requires evaluating it by hand.

Choosing a and b from π and r. The parameters a and b are not easy to read, so the curve is specified by its mean π and its r, and a and b are computed from them. It is a standard result, taken here without proof, that a beta distribution has mean a / (a + b) and variance ab / [(a + b) × (a + b) × (a + b + 1)]. The choice that gives mean π and variance r × π(1 − π) is

\[\begin{gathered} a \;=\; \pi\,\frac{1-r}{r}, \qquad b \;=\; (1-\pi)\,\frac{1-r}{r} \\[8pt] \text{which give}\qquad \operatorname{E}[p] = \pi, \qquad \operatorname{Var}[p] = r\,\pi(1-\pi) \end{gathered} \tag{11}\]
Box 6. Checking equation (11), and its values for the main bakery
  1. Add a and b. a + b = [π + (1 − π)] × (1 − r) / r = (1 − r) / r.
  2. The mean. a / (a + b): the factor (1 − r) / r cancels, leaving π. In the same way b / (a + b) = 1 − π.
  3. Add 1. a + b + 1 = (1 − r) / r + r / r = 1 / r.
  4. The variance. ab / [(a + b) × (a + b) × (a + b + 1)] is [a / (a + b)] × [b / (a + b)] divided by (a + b + 1). By lines 2 and 3 that is π × (1 − π) divided by 1 / r, which is r × π(1 − π), as equation (6) requires.
  5. The main bakery. a = 0.2 × (1 − 0.1) / 0.1 = 0.2 × 9 = 1.8, and b = 0.8 × 9 = 7.2. The curve in the left panel of Figure 2 is the beta distribution with a = 1.8 and b = 7.2.

Equation (11) cannot be used at r = 0 or r = 1 exactly, since it would divide by zero or give a = b = 0. Those two bakeries of Part 2 are not beta distributions; the beta family comes as close to either as one likes without reaching it. For r = 0 the binomial distribution itself is used.

The beta-binomial distribution: the count K. Put the two stages of Part 1 together with this curve. Stage 1: the tray draws its p from the beta distribution of equation (11). Stage 2: each of its n biscuits burns with that p, so that for a tray with known p the count is binomial. The distribution of K over both stages together is the beta-binomial distribution. In equation (12), k is any particular value the count might take, from 0 to n, and C(n, k) is the number of ways of choosing k items from n, as in equation (2).

\[\begin{gathered} P(K = k) \;=\; C(n,k)\;\frac{B(k+a,\; n-k+b)}{B(a,b)} \\[8pt] \operatorname{E}[K] = n\pi, \qquad \operatorname{Var}[K] = n\pi(1-\pi)\,\bigl[\,1+(n-1)\,r\,\bigr] \end{gathered} \tag{12}\]

The first line gives the probability of each count; it is a standard result and software evaluates it. The second line is nothing new: the mean is n times the average burn probability, and the variance is equation (8), which was derived in Part 4 without any reference to the beta distribution.

What the probabilities show. Figure 5 draws two distributions of K for trays of 50 with π = 0.2. Grey is the binomial: what the counts would be if all trays were alike. Blue is the beta-binomial for the main bakery, r = 0.1. Both have mean 10. Their SDs are the 2.8 and 6.9 of Box 4. Now the question that needed a shape can be answered. If trays were alike, a tray with 20 or more burnt biscuits would be almost impossible: probability 0.0009, about one tray in 1,100. In the main bakery it has probability 0.10: one ordinary tray in ten.

What that means for a comparison. Suppose the bakery tries a new recipe and wants to know whether it burns more easily than the usual one. It bakes one tray of the new recipe and finds 20 of the 50 biscuits burnt, where the usual recipe averages 10. That one tray does not show that the new recipe burns more: one tray in ten of the usual recipe does the same, because of a hot shelf. Several trays of the new recipe, each baked separately and all with many burnt biscuits, would show it, because independent trays are not all on a hot shelf by chance.

Common mistake: counting items as if each were separate evidence

It is tempting to reason: "I examined 50 biscuits of the new recipe and 20 were burnt. With the usual burn probability of 0.2, that many burnt biscuits has a probability below one in a thousand. So the new recipe burns more." The arithmetic is the binomial figure of 0.0009 and it is right. The conclusion is wrong, because the binomial treats 50 biscuits from one tray as 50 independent observations. By Part 5 they are worth 8.5, and the correct probability is one in ten. The same mistake with larger groups is the most common error in the kind of experiment this chapter is about: a thousand items from one group are not a thousand replicates.

From biscuits to nanowells. From section 3 on, the tray is a well, the biscuit is a nanowell, and "burnt" is "exceeds the cutoff". A well's shelf in the oven is everything its nanowells share: its seeding, its position on the plate, its medium, its imaging run. The new recipe is the treatment. Section 3 applies equations (6) to (12) to that case with n = 1,000 and π = 0.01, and section 4.1 builds a test on them.

2.4 Logarithms, odds and the logit

Logarithms. The number e is a constant, about 2.718. The natural logarithm of a positive number x, written ln(x), is the power to which e must be raised to give x. So ln(1) = 0, ln(2.718) = 1, and ln(0.01) = −4.61. The reverse operation is written ex. Two properties are used in this chapter. A logarithm turns multiplication into addition: for any two positive numbers u and v, ln(u × v) = ln(u) + ln(v). And it turns division into subtraction: ln(u / v) = ln(u) − ln(v). A logarithmic axis on a chart uses the same idea: equal distances along it stand for equal multiplications.

Odds. The odds of an event are its probability divided by the probability that it does not happen. A probability of 0.5 is odds of 0.5 / 0.5 = 1 ("even odds"). A probability of 0.01 is odds of 0.01 / 0.99 = 0.0101: one occurrence for every 99 non-occurrences. For a rare event the odds and the probability are nearly the same number.

The logit. The logit of a probability is the natural logarithm of its odds, also called the log-odds:

\[\operatorname{logit}(x) \;=\; \ln\!\left(\frac{x}{1-x}\right), \qquad \text{and back again:}\quad x \;=\; \frac{e^{y}}{1+e^{y}} \;\text{ for a logit } y \tag{13}\]

The logit of 0.01 is ln(0.0101) = −4.60. The logit of 0.5 is ln(1) = 0. Figure 6 shows the whole relationship.

Why the logit is useful. A probability must stay between 0 and 1, so a rule such as "add 0.02" or "multiply by 3" can push it out of range. A logit can be any number, so adding any amount to a logit always gives a valid probability. And because a logarithm turns multiplication into addition, adding a fixed amount to the logit multiplies the odds by a fixed factor, whatever the starting probability. The factor by which the odds are multiplied is called an odds ratio.

2.5 Counting arrangements

The factorial of a whole number n, written n!, is the product of all whole numbers from n down to 1: 4! = 4 × 3 × 2 × 1 = 24. It is the number of orders in which n different objects can be lined up.

The number of ways to choose k objects out of n, when the order of choosing does not matter, is written C(n, k):

\[C(n,k) \;=\; \frac{n!}{k!\,(n-k)!} \tag{14}\]
Worked example: choosing 3 wells out of 6

C(6, 3) = 6! / (3! × 3!) = 720 / (6 × 6) = 20. There are 20 different ways to pick which 3 of 6 wells receive a label.

2.6 Models, likelihood and maximum-likelihood estimation

The binomial distribution of section 2.1 works forwards: given a probability π, it says how likely each possible count is. Analysing an experiment runs the other way. The count has been observed, and π is what is unknown. Likelihood is the tool for working backwards from data to the unknown number behind it.

Models and parameters. A model is a mathematical description of how the data could have arisen. It contains one or more unknown numbers called parameters. "The number of heads in 10 tosses follows a binomial distribution" is a model, and its one parameter is π, the probability of heads on each toss. A model does not say what its parameters are. The data are used to find out.

The same formula, read in two directions. Take the binomial formula, equation (2), for 10 tosses.

  • Read as a probability: fix the parameter, say π = 0.5, and let the result vary. The formula gives the probability of 0 heads, 1 head, and so on up to 10. These add up to 1.
  • Read as a likelihood: fix the result at what was actually observed, say 7 heads, and let the parameter vary. The formula now gives, for each candidate value of π, the probability that a coin with that π would have produced the 7 heads that were seen.

The second reading is the likelihood of π. It is written L(π), and in general, for a model with parameters θ (θ may stand for one number or several):

\[L(\theta) \;=\; P(\text{the observed data} \mid \text{parameters } \theta), \qquad \log L(\theta) \;=\; \ln\bigl[L(\theta)\bigr] \tag{15}\]

The vertical bar is read "given": the probability of the observed data, given that the parameters have the value θ. The second part defines the log-likelihood, written log L: the natural logarithm of the likelihood, which is explained below. In "log L" the logarithm is always the natural one, ln.

Worked example: 7 heads in 10 tosses

A coin of unknown fairness gives 7 heads in 10 tosses. Equation (2) with n = 10 and k = 7 gives the likelihood L(π) = C(10, 7) × π7 × (1 − π)3, and C(10, 7) = 120. Try three candidate values:

  • π = 0.5: L = 120 × 0.57 × 0.53 = 0.117. A fair coin gives exactly 7 heads about 12 % of the time.
  • π = 0.7: L = 120 × 0.77 × 0.33 = 0.267. A coin that lands heads 70 % of the time gives exactly 7 heads about 27 % of the time.
  • π = 0.9: L = 120 × 0.97 × 0.13 = 0.057. A coin that lands heads 90 % of the time gives exactly 7 heads only about 6 % of the time; it would usually give more.

The observed result is more than twice as probable under π = 0.7 as under π = 0.5, and nearly five times as probable as under π = 0.9. In that sense the data support π = 0.7 best of the three.

Figure 7 draws the likelihood for every value of π between 0 and 1, with the three candidates marked. The curve says how well each possible π explains the 7 heads that were observed.

Common mistake: reading a likelihood as the probability of the parameter

A likelihood is not the probability that π has a particular value. The heights in Figure 7 do not add up to 1 over the possible values of π, and nothing requires them to. A likelihood is only meaningful in comparison: the ratio of two likelihoods says how many times better one parameter value explains the data than another. Its absolute size carries no meaning by itself, and it gets smaller as data accumulate, because any particular large data set is improbable under every model.

Maximum-likelihood estimation. The maximum-likelihood estimate (MLE) of a parameter is the value at which the likelihood is highest: the value under which the observed data were most probable. In Figure 7 the peak is at π = 0.7, which is simply the observed share, 7 out of 10. For a single binomial count the MLE of π is always the observed share k / n.

The observed share is a result anyone would have guessed. The value of the method is that it still works when no obvious guess exists. For a model with several parameters that interact, such as the one in section 4.1, there is no simple formula. A computer finds the MLE by search: it starts from trial values, computes the likelihood, adjusts the values in the direction that raises it, and repeats until no adjustment raises it further.

Several observations: likelihoods multiply. When the observations are independent, the probability of all of them together is the product of their separate probabilities, so the likelihood of a whole data set is the product of the likelihoods of its parts. With many observations that product becomes an extremely small number, awkward to compute with. Taking the logarithm solves this, because a logarithm turns a product into a sum (section 2.4): the log-likelihood of a data set is the sum of the log-likelihoods of its parts. The logarithm does not change where the peak is, since a larger likelihood always has a larger logarithm. So maximising log L gives the same estimate as maximising L. Log-likelihoods of probabilities are negative numbers, because the logarithm of a number below 1 is negative; a log-likelihood closer to 0 is a better fit.

Worked example: three wells sharing one probability

Three wells of 1,000 items each show 7, 9 and 14 items with some property, and a binomial model with a single shared π is proposed. The likelihood is the product of three binomial probabilities, so the log-likelihood is the sum of three logarithms. At π = 0.010 the three log-probabilities are −2.41, −2.07 and −2.96, a total of log L = −7.44. At π = 0.020 they are −7.64, −5.90 and −3.26, a total of log L = −16.80. The data are far better explained by π = 0.010. It is in fact the MLE: 7 + 9 + 14 = 30 items out of 3,000.

Comparing two models: the likelihood-ratio statistic. Likelihood also answers a second kind of question: does a more elaborate model explain the data better than a simpler one, by more than chance would allow? This is how a treatment effect is tested in section 4.1.

The two models must be nested: the simpler one is the fuller one with a parameter pinned to a fixed value. Fit both by maximum likelihood. The fuller model can never fit worse, because it could always set its extra parameter to the pinned value and reproduce the simpler model. So its maximised log-likelihood is at least as high. The question is whether it is higher by enough to matter. The likelihood-ratio statistic measures the gap:

\[D \;=\; 2\,\bigl(\log L_{\text{fuller model}} - \log L_{\text{simpler model}}\bigr) \tag{16}\]

It is called a ratio because a difference of logarithms is the logarithm of a ratio: D is twice the logarithm of the ratio of the two maximised likelihoods. D is 0 when the extra parameter does not help at all and grows as it becomes more necessary.

Check the extremes

If the extra parameter does not help at all, the fuller model's best fit is the simpler model itself, the two log-likelihoods are equal, and D = 0. If the simpler model cannot explain the data at all, its log-likelihood is hugely negative and D is very large. D is never negative, because the fuller model can always imitate the simpler one.

A general result of statistics says how large D gets by chance when the simpler model is in fact true and one parameter has been pinned: with enough data, the square root of D, given a plus sign when the estimated effect is in the direction being tested and a minus sign when it is not, behaves like a z from the standard normal curve, the bell curve with mean 0 and SD 1. A p-value can then be read from the normal tail area. The proof is beyond this chapter (the result is known as Wilks' theorem), but the reason can be sketched. Near its peak a log-likelihood curve is shaped like an upside-down parabola, as Figure 7's curve is near π = 0.7. On a parabola, the drop in height from the peak grows as the square of the distance from the peak. When distance is measured in units of the estimate's own SD, which is a z, the drop in log L works out to half of z × z. Doubling the drop and taking the square root therefore gives back z. That is where the 2 and the square root come from. "With enough data" is a real condition. With little data the result is only approximate. A second condition is that no estimated parameter sits at the edge of its allowed range; section 4.1 meets a case where one does.

Worked example: is the coin fair?

The simpler model pins π at 0.5 (a fair coin). The fuller model lets π be anything. For 7 heads in 10 tosses, the fuller model's maximised likelihood is L(0.7) = 0.267, so log L = ln(0.267) = −1.321. The simpler model has nothing to estimate: L(0.5) = 0.117, so log L = ln(0.117) = −2.144.

Equation (16) gives D = 2 × (−1.321 − (−2.144)) = 2 × 0.823 = 1.65. The estimate 0.7 is above 0.5, so z = +√1.65 = 1.28. The area of the standard normal curve above 1.28 is 0.10, and that is the approximate one-sided p-value. Seven heads in ten tosses is not strong evidence that a coin favours heads.

The exact answer can be computed here by adding the binomial probabilities of 7, 8, 9 and 10 heads for a fair coin: 0.17. The approximation gave 0.10. Ten tosses is too little data for the approximation to be accurate, and it erred on the optimistic side. The same caution applies whenever this method is used with few observations.

2.7 Simulation, shuffling and Monte Carlo p-values

Section 2.6 obtained a p-value from a formula: a general result says that, with enough data, a certain statistic follows the normal curve. Such results are approximations, and for many statistics no such result exists at all. The alternative is to build the reference distribution by computation. Any method that reaches its answer by generating many random or rearranged data sets is called a Monte Carlo method. Three are described here. The chapter relies on the first for two of its tests (sections 4.2 and 4.3) and on the third to check its main test (section 7).

What a p-value needs. A p-value compares the observed value of a test statistic with the values that statistic would take if the treatment did nothing. Statisticians call the supposition "the treatment does nothing" the null hypothesis, and that collection of values the statistic's null distribution. A formula is one way to know it. The methods below produce it directly, as a long list of values, and the p-value is then simply where the observed value falls in the list.

Method 1: shuffling the labels (a permutation test). Suppose some units received a treatment and others did not, and a statistic has been computed that compares the two groups. If the treatment truly did nothing, the labels "treated" and "control" carry no information: the same measurements would have arisen whichever units had been given which label. So every other way of assigning the same number of labels to the same units is an equally likely version of the experiment in which nothing is going on. The procedure is:

  1. Compute the statistic for the real labelling. Call it the observed value.
  2. Reassign the labels among the units in a different way, keeping the same number of each label, and recompute the statistic. Its value is one draw from the null distribution.
  3. Repeat step 2 for every possible assignment, or, when there are too many, for a large random sample of them.
  4. The p-value is the share of assignments whose statistic is at least as large as the observed value.

When every assignment is tried, the count includes the real one, and the p-value is exact:

\[p \;=\; \frac{\#\{\text{assignments with statistic} \ge \text{observed}\}}{\text{total number of assignments}} \tag{17}\]

where #{ } means "the number of". With c control and t treated units the total is C(c + t, t), by section 2.5. When only a random sample of shuffles is drawn, say S of them, the real assignment is added to the sample, which gives p = (1 + number of shuffles with statistic ≥ observed) / (S + 1).

Worked example: four control and four treated measurements

Four control units give 12, 15, 11 and 14 (mean 13). Four treated units give 18, 21, 16 and 25 (mean 20). The statistic is the difference of the means, treated minus control, and its observed value is 20 − 13 = 7.

There are C(8, 4) = 70 ways to choose which four of the eight measurements are called "treated". For each, the difference of means is recomputed. One of the 70, for example, calls 12, 15, 18 and 21 "treated": its difference is 16.5 − 16.5 = 0. Figure 8 shows all 70 values. They range from −7 to +7 and pile up near 0, as they should when labels are assigned without regard to the measurements.

Only one assignment, the real one, reaches a difference of 7. By equation (17), p = 1 / 70 = 0.014.

Three properties of this method matter later in the chapter.

  • It needs no formula and no assumed distribution. The statistic can be anything, however complicated. The only assumption is that, with no treatment effect, the units are interchangeable.
  • The units shuffled must be the independent ones. If measurements come in groups that share something, such as the nanowells of one well, the groups must be shuffled whole. Shuffling the individual measurements would break up what they share, make the null distribution too narrow, and make the p-value too small.
  • The p-value has a smallest possible value. The observed assignment can do no better than be the single most extreme, so p cannot be below 1 divided by the number of assignments: 1 / 70 here. Throughout this chapter a result is called significant when its p-value is strictly below 0.05. With few units the limit can be 0.05 or more, and then no result can be significant however strong the effect. This limit is called the floor of the design.

Method 2: simulating from a model. When a model of the data is available, the null distribution can be generated from it. Fit the model with the treatment effect set to zero. Use a computer to generate thousands of artificial data sets from that fitted model, compute the statistic on each, and read the p-value off the resulting list in the same way as in step 4 above. Unlike shuffling, this has no floor, because any number of artificial data sets can be generated. Its weakness is that it is only as good as the model and its fitted parameters: if the fitted model understates how much the data vary, the simulated null distribution is too narrow and the p-value is too small.

Method 3: checking a method by simulation. Simulation can also measure how well a test behaves. Generate thousands of artificial data sets in which the truth is known, apply the test to each, and count how often it gives each answer. If the artificial data contain no effect, the share called significant is the test's false-positive rate. If they contain an effect of a set size, it is the test's power for that size. The coin example of section 2.6 shows why this matters: an approximate p-value of 0.10 stood where the exact value was 0.17. For a coin the exact value can be worked out directly. For most tests it cannot, and simulation is then the only way to find such a gap.

What none of these can do. Rearranging or regenerating data creates no new information. A shuffle re-uses the units that were measured, and a simulation re-uses what those units told the model. Neither can make up for too few independent units: a thousand measurements inside one well describe that well very precisely and say nothing about how the next well would differ.

3. How much exceedance counts vary between untreated wells

Where we areSection 1 reduced the problem to one question: is a treated well's exceedance count more than chance allows? Section 2.3 showed that a count taken in groups varies for two reasons and that the second, differences between groups, is governed by r. This section applies that result to wells of 1,000 nanowells, puts numbers on it, and shows how r is estimated. No treatment appears anywhere in it. Two changes of wording start here. The "group" of section 2.3 is from now on always called a well, and the word group is kept for a set of wells that received the same treatment (the control group, the treated group). And a well's own exceedance probability, written p for a tray in section 2.3, is written πi from now on, because the letter p is needed for p-values.

A treated well's count means something only next to how much untreated wells' counts vary on their own. This section sizes that variation. Nothing in it involves a treatment.

3.1 Why the spread matters, not the average

Three symbols are used from here on, all for a single well. n is the number of nanowells in the well, 1,000 in every example from here on unless another number is stated. K is the number of those n nanowells that are exceedances, that is, whose FC is above the cutoff; every well has its own K. π is the probability that an untreated nanowell is an exceedance, which is 0.01 because the cutoff is the controls' 99th percentile.

An untreated well is expected to have K = nπ = 1,000 × 0.01 = 10. Because that expectation is the same for every untreated well, it cannot say whether a treated well's K of 20 is unusual. What decides it is how often an untreated well's K reaches 20 by itself, and that is set by how widely K scatters around 10 from one untreated well to the next. The spread is measured by the variance of K (the average squared distance from 10; its unit is counts squared) or by its square root, the standard deviation (SD, in counts).

3.2 The two sources of spread, and where the formula comes from

Number the nanowells of a well 1 to n, and let Xj be 1 if nanowell j is an exceedance and 0 if not. Then K = X1 + … + Xn, and its variance follows from three facts:

  • Each Xj is a yes-or-no outcome with probability π. The variance of such an outcome is π(1 − π), by equation (1).
  • The variance of a sum is the sum of the n individual variances, plus the covariance of every ordered pair of different nanowells, by the general rule for the variance of a sum in section 2.2. There are n(n − 1) such pairs.
  • Let r be the correlation between Xj and Xk for two nanowells of the same well, the intra-well correlation. In the notation of equation (4), r = Corr[Xj, Xk]. It is the same r as in section 2.3, with wells as the groups and nanowells as the items. By equation (4), a covariance is the correlation times the two SDs. Both SDs here are √(π(1 − π)), so the covariance of each pair is r · π(1 − π).

Adding the n variances, nπ(1 − π) in total, and the n(n − 1) covariances, n(n − 1) · r · π(1 − π) in total, gives equation (18). It is a sum of two terms, one for each source of spread. Because nπ(1 − π) appears in both terms, the sum can also be written as a single product, the last form in the equation. The two forms are the same quantity; the product is only shorthand. It is the beta-binomial variance of equation (12), with wells as the groups and nanowells as the items.

\[\operatorname{Var}[K] \;=\; \underbrace{n\pi(1-\pi)}_{\substack{\text{counting noise}\\ \text{within a well } (9.9)}} \;+\; \class{eqb}{\underbrace{n\pi(1-\pi)\,(n-1)\,r}_{\substack{\text{wells differing}\\ (98.9 \text{ at } r = 0.01)}}} \;=\; n\pi(1-\pi)\,\bigl[\,1+(n-1)\,r\,\bigr] \tag{18}\]

Both terms describe random variation among untreated wells. Neither contains any treatment effect.

Why a correlation inside a well is labelled "variation between wells". These are one fact seen from two positions, because nanowells of a well are correlated only when wells differ. Suppose half of all untreated wells had an exceedance rate of 0 % and half had 2 %, an average of 1 %. Seen across wells, the rates differ: that is variation between wells. Seen inside a well, finding one exceedance reveals that the well is a 2 % well, so its neighbouring nanowell has a 2 % chance of exceeding, not the 1 % average: that is correlation between nanowells of the same well. If every well were exactly 1 %, one exceedance would reveal nothing about its neighbours, and the correlation would be 0.

In general, the covariance between two nanowells of the same well equals the variance of the wells' own rates. So r is the correlation between nanowells within a well, and r · π(1 − π) is the variance of the exceedance rate from well to well. Different wells are assumed independent of one another: r describes how much wells differ, not any tendency for wells to move together.

With n = 1,000 and π = 0.01, the two terms mean the following.

  • First term, random variation within a well: nπ(1 − π) = 9.9. This is counting noise. Even if a well's exceedance probability were fixed at exactly 1 %, which of its nanowells happen to exceed is a matter of chance, so the number that do varies from well to well, just as 1,000 tosses of the same coin do not give the same number of heads each time. It is all that remains when r = 0, that is, when every nanowell is independent of every other. K is then an ordinary binomial count of rare events (section 2.1), and its variance is close to its mean. The SD is √9.9 ≈ 3.1 counts.
  • Second term, random variation between wells: nπ(1 − π) · (n − 1) · r. Nanowells of one well share everything that was done to that well: the same nutrient liquid, the same batch of cells placed in it, the same position on the plate (wells at the edge dry out faster), and the same session at the microscope. A well that happens to run fast lifts all of its nanowells together, so they are positively correlated and r > 0. An equivalent way to read r: each well has its own exceedance probability πi, and πi varies from well to well around π with variance r · π(1 − π). At r = 0.01 that is an SD of 1.0 percentage point around the 1 % mean; at r = 0.001 it is 0.3 points.
  • Why a small r has a large effect. Each nanowell is correlated with all n − 1 = 999 others in its well, so in the second term r is multiplied by 999. Compare two untreated wells of 1,000 nanowells. If nanowells are independent (r = 0), the second term is 0: the variance of K is 9.9 + 0 = 9.9 and its SD is 3.1 counts. If r = 0.01, the second term is 9.9 × 999 × 0.01 = 98.9: the variance of K is 9.9 + 98.9 = 108.8 and its SD is 10.4 counts. In the product form, that is 9.9 × (1 + 9.99) = 9.9 × 10.99, the same number. A correlation of only 0.01 between nanowells makes the SD of a well's count more than three times as large.

Neither term contains a treatment effect. Both describe how untreated wells differ from one another by chance: the first because a finite number of nanowells is counted, the second because no two wells are handled identically. A treatment enters only later, as a shift in the average exceedance rate of the treated wells (section 4.1). The variance above is the yardstick that shift is measured against.

Figure 9 shows the second source directly. It plots how untreated wells' own rates πi are distributed for two values of r. Both distributions average 1 %. At r = 0.001 about 19 wells in 20 lie between 0.5 % and 1.7 %. At r = 0.01 about one untreated well in five has a rate below 0.2 % and about one in seven has a rate above 2 %, with no treatment anywhere.

3.3 What the spread means for a count of 20

The variance can be turned into the probability of each possible count. With counting noise alone (r = 0), K follows the binomial distribution of section 2.1. With both sources, each well first draws its own rate πi from the distribution of Figure 9 and then counts with that rate, and K follows the beta-binomial distribution of section 2.3, with wells as the groups and nanowells as the items.

Figure 10 plots both for an untreated well: for each count K, the share of untreated wells expected to show it. Under the binomial (grey), 20 or more exceedances occur in 0.3 % of untreated wells, so a treated well at 20 would stand out. Under the beta-binomial with r = 0.01 (blue), 20 or more occur in 15 % of untreated wells, about one in seven, so one treated well at 20, taken alone, is weak evidence of a treatment.

Replicate treated wells are what restore the evidence. Suppose the treatment does nothing. Then the treated wells are simply more untreated wells, and untreated wells are independent of one another, so the chance that several all reach 20 is the product of the single-well chances. At r = 0.01 that is 0.15 × 0.15 = 2 % for two wells and 0.15 × 0.15 × 0.15 = 0.3 % for three. Three treated wells that all reach 20 are therefore as hard to explain by chance as a single well would be if wells did not differ. This is why every test in section 4 judges the treated wells as a group, and why the number of replicate wells, not the number of nanowells, sets what can be concluded.

3.4 Which source dominates depends on r, and r must be measured

Figure 11 draws the variance of K to scale for three values of r. The grey part, counting noise within a well, is 9.9 in every bar. The blue part, wells differing, is 9.9 × (n − 1) × r: one tenth of the grey at r = 0.0001, equal to it at r = 0.001, and ten times it at r = 0.01. Over a range of r that cannot be told apart by intuition, the yardstick changes tenfold.

r is not known in advance and differs between experiments, so it is estimated from the wells themselves. The principle: within a group of replicate wells, compare how much the counts actually disagree with how much they would disagree from counting noise alone. Any excess is attributed to wells differing, and r is the size of that excess.

Worked example: four control wells with 7, 8, 14 and 11 exceedances out of 1,000
  1. Observed scatter. The mean count is 10. The variance of the four counts around it is (9 + 4 + 16 + 1) / 3 = 10.0, where the four numbers added are the squares of the distances of 7, 8, 14 and 11 from 10 (3, 2, 4 and 1), and the divisor is the number of wells minus one. (Section 3.1 described a variance as an average of squared distances. When the mean itself has been worked out from the same few values, as here, the sum of squares is divided by one less than the number of values. Dividing by 4 would understate the scatter, because the four values are, by construction, closer to their own average than to the true mean.)
  2. Scatter expected from counting noise alone. nπ(1 − π) = 9.9, the first term of equation (18), with π = 10 / 1,000.
  3. The ratio. 10.0 / 9.9 = 1.01: these wells scatter 1 % more than counting noise alone gives on average.
  4. Solve for r. Dividing equation (18) by its first term shows that this ratio equals 1 + (n − 1)r, so r = (1.01 − 1) / 999 ≈ 0.00001. That is essentially 0: these four wells scatter almost exactly as counting noise alone makes them, and show no real sign of differing beyond it. Had the ratio been 1.31, the same step would have given r = 0.31 / 999 ≈ 0.0003. A ratio below 1 would need an r below 0, which wells that differ cannot produce, and r is then set to 0.

Three points about this estimate matter in practice.

  • Each group is measured around its own mean. Treated wells that are all higher than the controls do not raise r. Only disagreement among wells of the same group does.
  • It is imprecise with few wells. Four wells give only three independent pieces of information about scatter: the four distances from the average always add up to zero, so once three are known the fourth is fixed. Four other wells drawn from the same population could easily give a ratio well above 1, and an r to match, or one below 1, where these gave 1.01 (section 4.1 returns to this).
  • It comes out too small more often than too large. The scatter seen among a handful of wells is usually somewhat less than the true scatter and occasionally much more, so most experiments underestimate it. And whenever the wells happen to agree more closely than counting noise predicts, the ratio falls below 1 and r is set to 0, as it is for the treated wells of the example plate in section 4.1, which amounts to saying that wells do not differ at all. In both cases the yardstick is too short, which favours false detections. With only two control wells there is a single piece of information about scatter, too little to estimate r from at all; that is why three is the minimum, and why every well beyond three makes the result more reliable.

3.5 More nanowells per well cannot replace more wells

Divide K by n to get a well's exceedance rate, the share of its nanowells above the cutoff. Dividing a quantity by a constant divides its SD by that constant, and its variance by the constant times itself, because a variance is measured in squared units. For example, halving every count halves the SD and quarters the variance. So the variance of K / n is the variance of K, equation (18), divided by n × n. Dividing each term of equation (18) by n × n and simplifying gives two terms:

\[\operatorname{Var}\!\left[\frac{K}{n}\right] \;=\; \frac{\operatorname{Var}[K]}{n \times n} \;=\; \frac{\pi(1-\pi)}{n} \;+\; r\,\pi(1-\pi)\,\frac{n-1}{n} \tag{19}\]

The first term is the counting noise within a well (section 3.2): the chance scatter in how many of a well's nanowells happen to exceed. It shrinks toward zero as n grows, because a rate measured on more nanowells is measured more precisely. The second, wells differing, approaches r · π(1 − π) and stays there however many nanowells are counted. Figure 12 plots the SD of each part against n for r = 0.01: the total SD is 1.40 points at 100 nanowells per well, 1.04 at 1,000 and 1.00 at 10,000. At this r, past about 1,000 nanowells per well it barely falls; with a smaller r the levelling-off comes later, near 1 / r nanowells. Only averaging over more wells reduces the between-well part, because the variance of the average of several wells is the single-well variance divided by the number of wells.

Common mistake: buying precision with nanowells

Doubling the nanowells per well feels like doubling the data. It halves the counting-noise part of the variance of each well's rate and leaves the part due to differences between wells untouched. At r = 0.01 a well of 1,000 nanowells is worth 91 independent nanowells and a well of 2,000 is worth 95 (equation (9)). Two wells of 1,000 are worth 182.

Key points of section 3
  • An untreated well of 1,000 nanowells expects 10 exceedances. What matters is how far a well strays from 10 by chance.
  • The variance of the count is counting noise, 9.9, plus a term for wells differing, 9.9 × 999 × r. The two add.
  • At r = 0.01 a count of 20 or more occurs in 15 % of untreated wells, against 0.3 % if wells did not differ. One high well is weak evidence; several high wells are strong evidence.
  • r is estimated from how far replicate wells disagree beyond counting noise. With three or four wells the estimate is imprecise and tends to be too small.
  • Once a well has many more than 1 / r nanowells (100 at r = 0.01), more nanowells barely help. More wells do.

4. Four tests for an excess of high-FC nanowells in treated wells

Where we areSection 3 supplied the yardstick: how much untreated wells' counts vary. This section builds tests that hold treated wells against that yardstick. The first, the exceedance test, is the one that decides, and it is developed in seven steps. The other three are shorter, because they reuse ideas already met.

All four tests ask the same question of the upper tail: do treated wells hold more high-FC nanowells than untreated wells do? They differ in what they measure and in how they decide whether the answer is beyond chance. The p-values of the first three tests are one-sided, for an excess in the treated wells. The fourth is a standard two-sided test, one that responds to a difference in either direction, and is reported only as a description.

4.1 Exceedance test PRIMARY

What it measures. For each well, the count K of nanowells above the cutoff (Figure 13A). The test asks whether the treated wells' exceedance rates are higher, as a group, than the control wells' rates, by more than the spread between wells allows. The seven steps below build it from the start; the example plate's eight wells are used throughout.

Step 1. Keep three quantities apart: the cutoff, a well's underlying rate, and its observed rate. The cutoff is one fold-change value, fixed by the controls: the 99th percentile of all control nanowells pooled. Across the control wells together, exactly 1 % of nanowells exceed it. A well's underlying rate, written πi, is the probability that one of its own nanowells exceeds that fixed cutoff. It is not exactly 1 % in every well, because wells differ (section 3.2), and it is never observed directly. What is observed is the well's exceedance rate, K / n (section 3.5), which is πi blurred by counting noise. Whenever "rate" appears below, the text says which of the two is meant. On the example plate the four control wells hold 7, 8, 14 and 11 exceedances out of 1,000: observed rates of 0.7 %, 0.8 %, 1.4 % and 1.1 %, which average to exactly 1.0 %, the share the cutoff leaves above it by construction.

Going deeper: counting a control well against the other control wells

Setting the cutoff from the same control wells that are then counted has a side effect. The control counts are forced to add up to exactly 1 % of all control nanowells (40 of 4,000 with four control wells), so they cannot vary as freely as four independent wells would, and their scatter slightly understates r. A refinement removes it: count each control well against a cutoff set by the other control wells only, so that no well helps set the bar it is measured against, while treated wells are still counted against the cutoff from all the control wells. On the example plate, counting each control well against the other three changes the control counts from 7, 8, 14 and 11 to 7, 8, 14 and 13; the treated counts stay the same. The difference is small, and the worked examples in this section keep the one pooled cutoff of FC 2.2 and the counts 7, 8, 14 and 11, so that there is one cutoff to think about. The simulation of section 7 uses the refinement.

Step 2. Describe how one well's count arises. The model imagines each well's count being produced in two stages. These are the two stages of Figure 2, with a well in place of a tray and a nanowell in place of a biscuit.

  1. The well draws its own rate πi at random from a distribution of possible rates. The distribution used is the beta distribution of section 2.3, a standard family of curves for quantities that lie between 0 and 1; Figure 9 shows two of them. Its centre is the group's mean rate, and its width is set by r.
  2. Each of the well's n nanowells then exceeds the cutoff or not, independently, with probability πi. The resulting count K follows the binomial distribution.

A count produced by these two stages together follows the beta-binomial distribution (sections 2.3 and 3.3). The model does not give each well a rate of its own to be estimated. With one free rate per well, every count would be explained perfectly and nothing would be left to compare. Only the centre and width of each group's distribution are estimated.

Step 3. Put rates on a scale where a treatment effect is one number: odds and the logit. The two groups are not compared by subtracting their rates. They are compared through their odds.

  • Odds. Section 2.4 introduced odds and the logit; they are restated here because the model depends on them. The odds of an event are its probability divided by the probability that it does not happen: odds = x / (1 − x). A rate of 1 % is odds of 0.01 / 0.99 ≈ 0.0101, one exceedance for every 99 nanowells that are not. A rate of 50 % is odds of 1.
  • Logit. The logit of a rate is the natural logarithm of its odds: logit(x) = ln(x / (1 − x)). It is also called the log-odds. The logit of 1 % is ln(0.0101) = −4.60; the logit of 50 % is ln(1) = 0. To go back from a logit y to a rate, use x = ey / (1 + ey).
  • Why this scale. A rate must stay between 0 and 1, so a rule such as "add 2 points" or "multiply by 3" can push it out of range. A logit can be any number from −∞ to +∞, so adding any amount to it always gives a valid rate. And adding the same amount always multiplies the odds by the same factor, whatever rate it starts from.

Step 4. Write the model as one equation. Let "treated" be 0 for a control well and 1 for a treated well. The centre of each group's distribution of rates is given by:

\[\operatorname{logit}\bigl(\text{mean of } \pi_i\bigr) \;=\; \alpha + \delta \cdot \text{treated} \tag{20}\]
  • For a control well (treated = 0) the right-hand side is α. So α is the logit of the control group's mean rate.
  • For a treated well (treated = 1) the right-hand side is α + δ. So δ is the amount added to the logit by treatment: the difference between the two groups' log-odds. It is called the gap in the rest of this section. (Many texts write these two numbers as β₀ and β₁. This chapter writes α and δ, so that neither is mistaken for the beta distribution of section 2.3, and so that the 0 of β₀ is not read as "no effect".)
  • The baseline is not the "no effect" fit. α is the control group's level. It is estimated in every fit, including the one in step 6 that forbids a gap. "No effect" is a statement about the gap, δ = 0, never about α.
  • The sign of the gap is its direction. δ above 0 means the treated wells are higher than the control wells (an odds ratio above 1). δ = 0 means no difference (an odds ratio of exactly 1). δ below 0 means the treated wells are lower (an odds ratio below 1).
  • The odds ratio. A difference of two logarithms is the logarithm of a ratio, so δ = ln(treated odds / control odds), and eδ = treated odds / control odds. This ratio is the odds ratio. An odds ratio of 1 (δ = 0) means the treatment changes nothing; 2 means the odds of a nanowell exceeding are doubled. For rare events the odds and the rate are nearly the same number, so here the odds ratio is close to the plain ratio of the two exceedance rates.

The model therefore has three unknown numbers: α (the control group's level), δ (the treatment effect) and r (how much wells differ by chance; section 3.4).

Why one r for both groups. The model assumes that treated wells differ among themselves about as much as control wells do, and fits a single r to both. This is an assumption, made out of necessity: a group of two, three or four wells carries far too little information to estimate its own r; the note on r below shows what such groups return. It would be wrong for a treatment that makes wells erratic, and the result should be read with that in mind.

Wells on more than one plate. If the wells being compared sit on different plates, a whole plate can run faster or slower than another. The equation then gets one extra number for each additional plate, added to the logit of every well on that plate, so that a difference between plates is not mistaken for a difference between treatments. The examples in this chapter use a single plate and need no such term.

Step 5. Find the three numbers: maximum likelihood. This is maximum-likelihood estimation, the method of section 2.6, applied to the beta-binomial model. That section worked it for a coin; here the data are the counts of all the wells and there are three parameters instead of one. For any trial values of α, δ and r, the beta-binomial distribution gives the probability of each well's observed count. Multiplying those probabilities over all wells gives the probability of the whole set of observed counts under those trial values. That probability is called the likelihood, and its logarithm, the log-likelihood, is written log L. A higher log L means the trial values explain the data better. The fit searches for the values with the highest log L. These are the maximum-likelihood estimates: the values under which the observed counts were most probable.

Common mistake: confusing log-odds with log-likelihood

Both are logarithms and both are negative numbers here, but they describe different things. Rates, odds and log-odds describe the nanowells: how often a nanowell exceeds the cutoff. α and δ are log-odds, so they are statements about the biology. A likelihood describes a guess: how probable the observed counts would be if that guess of α, δ and r were true. The counts stay fixed and the guess changes. The log-likelihood is therefore a score for one complete guess, and it is the tool used to find α and δ, not another name for them. On the example plate, α = −4.60 says where the control group sits; log L = −20.25 says how well the whole fit explains the eight counts.

Step 6. Ask whether the treatment effect is needed: fit the model twice.

  • With a treatment effect: α, δ and r are all free. Call the highest log-likelihood reached log Lδ free.
  • Without one: δ is forced to 0, so both groups must share one mean rate, and only α and r are free. Call the highest log-likelihood reached log Lδ = 0. It can never be higher than log Lδ free, because the second fit has less freedom.

The test statistic is twice the gap between them, the likelihood-ratio statistic of equation (16) applied to this model:

\[D \;=\; 2\,\bigl(\log L_{\delta\ \text{free}} - \log L_{\delta = 0}\bigr) \tag{21}\]

D is 0 when allowing a treatment effect does not help at all, and grows as the effect becomes more necessary to explain the counts. The spread between wells enters here. In the fit without an effect, the only way to explain treated wells that are higher than control wells is to say that wells differ a great deal, that is, to raise r. If the data allow a large r, that explanation works nearly as well as a treatment effect, the gap is small, and D stays small. That is how the spread between wells protects against false detections.

The gap and the spread answer two separate questions. δ asks whether one group is higher than the other. r asks whether wells of the same group disagree with one another by more than counting noise. r is measured around each group's own mean, after the gap has been taken out, so a large gap does not, by itself, put anything into r. Figure 14 shows the four combinations, each with the fitted gap and the fitted r.

r is estimated in both fits; it is never fixed. Neither fit is told what r should be. Each finds the r under which the counts are most probable. On the example plate that is r ≈ 0 when a gap is allowed and r = 0.0027 when it is forbidden. (The first estimate sits at 0, the lowest value r can take: around their own group means, the wells scatter about as much as counting noise alone would make them, or less.) The wells have not changed. What changed is that the difference between the groups, no longer allowed to be a gap, has only one other place to go: it is re-read as wells differing from one another.

Common mistake: thinking a simpler fit always reports more spread

r rises in the second fit only when there is a gap for that fit to forbid. Figure 14 shows both cases. Where the groups really differ (top row), forbidding the gap raises r: from about 0 to 0.0027, and from 0.0055 to 0.0086. Where they do not (bottom row), it changes little or nothing: about 0 in both fits, and 0.0079 to 0.0091. In the bottom-right plot one well at 64 needs a large r in either fit, because a gap would have to lift all four treated wells and three of them did not move. How much r must rise when the gap is forbidden is another view of how much the gap was needed.

How large is the gain when the treatment does nothing? The fit with a gap can never score lower than the fit without, because δ = 0 is one of its options. A higher log-likelihood is therefore not evidence by itself. The question is whether it is higher by more than chance alone would give. Figure 15 answers that directly: 1,500 plates of 4 control and 4 treated wells were simulated with no treatment effect, and both fits were run on each. The simulated wells were made to differ as much as the example plate's fit without a gap says they do: every well drew its own rate around a mean of 1.7 % with r = 0.0027. The gain is usually a few tenths of a unit; 95 % of the plates gain less than 2.57. The example plate gains 7.12. None of the 1,500 gained as much, in either direction: the largest gain among them, 6.90, came from a plate whose treated wells were lower than its controls. Step 7 replaces this simulation with a formula. The formula puts the 95 % point at 1.92, not 2.57: with four and four wells that differ a little, chance gains are larger than the formula allows, so its p-values come out somewhat too small. Of these 1,500 plates, 8.1 % passed z = 1.64, where the formula says 5 %.

Check the extremes

Treated and control wells look alike. The best-fitting δ is near 0, the two fits are nearly the same, and D is near 0. No evidence.

Treated wells are all far above control wells, and wells within each group agree. Without a treatment effect the fit must stretch one curve over both groups, which fits badly; with one it fits well. D is large.

Treated wells are higher on average, but wells within each group disagree wildly. The fit learns a large r. A single wide curve then covers all the wells almost as well as two curves do, and D stays small. This is the case that a test ignoring r would get wrong.

Step 7. Turn D into a p-value. A p-value needs to know how large D gets by chance when the treatment truly does nothing. A general result of statistics supplies that: with enough data, the signed square root

\[z \;=\; \operatorname{sign}(\delta)\,\sqrt{D}, \qquad p \;=\; 1 - \Phi(z) \tag{22}\]

then behaves like a draw from the standard normal distribution, the bell curve with mean 0 and SD 1. The sign of δ is needed because D has no direction: it measures only how much the gap helps to explain the counts, and treated wells that were lower than the controls by the same amount would give the same D. Attaching the sign of δ makes z positive when treated wells are higher and negative when they are lower. A negative z gives a p-value above 0.5, so a treatment that lowers the count can never be called. The p-value is the share of the bell curve lying above the observed z. That is the second part of equation (22), where Φ(z) is the share of the curve lying below z. It is one-sided: only an excess in treated wells counts as evidence. A z of 1.64 gives p = 0.05; a z of 2.33 gives p = 0.01. "With enough data" is the weak point: a handful of wells is not much data, and section 7 measures how well the approximation holds. The result also assumes that r is not at the edge of its range, and on the example plate r is estimated at exactly 0, which is the edge.

Common mistake: reading the p-value as the chance the treatment does nothing

A p-value of 0.00008 does not mean there is a 0.008 % chance that the treatment has no effect. It means: if the treatment had no effect, counts this lopsided would arise 0.008 % of the time, on about 8 plates in 100,000. That figure comes from the formula of step 7, which is too optimistic for so few wells; Figure 15 and section 7 suggest the true rate is a few times higher. The first statement is about the treatment; the second is about the data. The difference matters most when many treatments are tested, because then some small p-values are certain to arise from treatments that do nothing (section 5).

Two numbers come out, and they answer different questions. The odds ratio is the effect size: how large the excess is. The p-value says how often an excess this large would arise by chance if the treatment did nothing. A small excess measured on many consistent wells can have a small p-value, and a large excess on few or inconsistent wells can have a large one, so both are needed.

Why it is the test that decides. It uses every well's count, it treats the well as the replicate through r, and its p-value has no lower limit set by the number of wells. Its limits are two. It needs at least 3 control and 2 treated wells, because with fewer there is too little information to estimate r. And its p-value rests on the large-sample approximation of step 7, applied to a handful of wells.

4.2 Higher Criticism (HC) SUPPORTING

What it measures. Whether a well has more extreme nanowells than chance allows, without committing to one cutoff in advance. The exceedance test fixes its cutoff at the 99th percentile. A responder that lands just below it, at the 98th percentile, is not counted at all. A responder far above it, at the 99.99th, counts for no more than a nanowell that barely clears the line. HC scans all thresholds in the tail and reports the one where the excess is clearest.

Step 1: a p-value for every nanowell. Let N be the number of control nanowells in the pool. Each nanowell of the well under test gets

\[p_i \;=\; \frac{1 + \#\{\text{pooled control nanowells with FC} \ge \text{this nanowell's}\}}{N + 1} \tag{23}\]

where #{ } means "the number of". This is the share of control nanowells at least as high as it. The 1 added to top and bottom keeps p above 0. A control well is scored against the other control wells, never against a pool that contains itself. If the well is like the controls, its p-values are spread evenly between 0 and 1: about 1 % of them below 0.01, about 0.1 % below 0.001, and so on.

Step 2: compare observed with expected at every threshold. Sort the well's p-values from smallest: p(1) ≤ p(2) ≤ …. Take the i-th smallest as a threshold, written τ (the Greek letter tau; the letter t is kept for the number of treated wells). By construction, i nanowells have p ≤ τ. Chance alone would give n · τ of them, with an SD of √(n · τ(1 − τ)), the binomial SD of section 2.1. The standardised excess is (observed − expected) / SD, and HC is its largest value. In equation (24), "max over i" means: work the fraction out for every i in turn, and keep the largest result.

\[\mathrm{HC} \;=\; \max_{i}\; \frac{i - n\,p_{(i)}}{\sqrt{n\,p_{(i)}\bigl(1 - p_{(i)}\bigr)}} \tag{24}\]

In words. Try a threshold. Count how many of the well's nanowells are at least as extreme as it. Subtract the number chance would put there, and divide by the usual scatter of that number. That is the excess at this threshold, in SDs. Now try every threshold, and keep the largest excess found.

The maximum is searched only over the most extreme 10 % of the well's nanowells, where a rare effect lives, and skips thresholds at or below 1/n (0.001 for a well of 1,000 nanowells), where the denominator is so small that a single nanowell could dominate.

What "in SDs" does and does not mean here. The SD in the formula is the binomial one, which section 3 showed is too small when wells differ. HC is therefore a score for ranking wells, not a z that can be looked up in a normal table: an HC of 3 is not "three SDs, so one chance in a thousand". Its meaning comes entirely from the comparison with other wells, set out under "p-value" below. The name, incidentally, is historical: it comes from a phrase the statistician John Tukey used for judging many p-values at once.

Reading Figure 13B. The curve is the standardised excess at each threshold. Control well C1 stays within about 2 SDs of 0 everywhere; its largest value, at a threshold near 0.0013, is 2.3. Treated well T1 holds 21 nanowells above the cutoff, and most of them are higher than almost every control nanowell: 8 are higher than all 4,000. At the threshold τ = 0.00125, 14 of its nanowells have p ≤ τ where chance expects 1,000 × 0.00125 = 1.25, with an SD of √(1.25 × 0.999) = 1.12. That is an excess of (14 − 1.25) / 1.12 = 11.4 SDs, the largest at any threshold the scan admits, so HC = 11.4. As the threshold loosens, those few high nanowells are diluted by ordinary ones and the curve falls back toward 0. A few strong responders produce exactly this shape, which is why HC suits sparse effects.

Check the extremes

A well exactly like the controls. Observed and expected agree at every threshold, every excess is near 0, and HC is small. Whether a treated well's HC is beyond chance is settled only by comparing it with what untreated wells produce, which is the next step.

A well in which every nanowell is shifted up a little. The excess is modest at every threshold and largest at loose ones. HC is moderate. This is the shape test's territory, not HC's.

A well with a few very extreme nanowells. The excess is enormous at strict thresholds and gone at loose ones. HC is large. This is what it was built for.

p-value: relabelling whole wells. HC has no reliable textbook distribution when wells differ, so its p-value is built from the experiment's own wells, by the shuffling method of section 2.7 with the well as the unit that is shuffled. The argument is: if the treatment did nothing, the labels "treated" and "control" are arbitrary, and any other assignment of the same labels to the same wells was equally likely to produce the result. So every possible assignment is tried. For each, the wells labelled "treated" are scored against the wells labelled "control", and the mean HC of the "treated" wells is recorded. The p-value is the share of assignments whose mean is at least as large as the real one:

\[p \;=\; \frac{\#\{\text{labellings with mean HC} \ge \text{the real labelling's}\}}{C(c+t,\,t)} \tag{25}\]

C(c + t, t) is the number of distinct ways to choose which t of the c + t wells are called "treated":

\[C(c+t,\,t) \;=\; \frac{(c+t)!}{c!\;t!} \tag{26}\]

The exclamation mark is the factorial of section 2.5: 4! = 4 × 3 × 2 × 1 = 24. With 4 control and 4 treated wells, equation (26) gives 8! / (4! × 4!) = 40,320 / 576 = 70. Whole wells are relabelled, never individual nanowells, so everything the nanowells of a well share stays with them and is part of what chance can produce.

Figure 17 shows all 70 labellings of the example plate. The four treated wells have HC of 11.4, 11.0, 15.9 and 14.3, a mean of 13.2, and no other labelling gives a mean as high, so p = 1/70 = 0.014. That is below 0.05, so the result is significant. The 16 labellings that call three of the four real treated wells "treated" come next, with means between 3.4 and 5.4; every other labelling gives 2.8 or less. No labelling can do better than be the single most extreme, so 1/70 is the smallest p this design can ever give, however strong the effect. That limit is the floor introduced in section 2.7; section 4.6 tabulates it for other designs, some of which cannot reach significance at all.

Common mistake: shuffling nanowells in place of wells

With 8,000 nanowells on the plate it is tempting to shuffle those, since they allow an astronomically large number of rearrangements and so a p-value as small as one likes. That destroys exactly what makes wells differ: after such a shuffle every "well" is a random mix of all eight, so the shuffled wells agree with one another far better than real wells ever do. The null distribution comes out too narrow and the p-value too small. Shuffling whole wells keeps each well's nanowells together, and the floor of 1/70 is the honest price of having only eight wells.

4.3 Shape test (upper-tail Anderson–Darling) SUPPORTING

What it measures. How much of a well's distribution sits higher than the controls', anywhere above the control median. Let F0(x) be the pooled controls' cumulative curve, the share of control nanowells with FC ≤ x, and Fn(x) the same for the well under test. Where Fn lies below F0, a smaller share of the well is at or below that FC, so a larger share is above it: the shaded gap in Figure 13C. The statistic adds up those gaps. Equation (27) is written with an integral sign, ∫, which stands for a sum taken over a continuous range. Its meaning here, for a reader who has not met integrals, is spelled out directly after it.

\[A^2 \;=\; n \int \frac{\bigl[\max(F_0 - F_n,\,0)\bigr]^2}{1 - F_0}\; dF_0 \qquad \text{over FC above the control median} \tag{27}\]

In words. Walk up the fold-change scale from the control median. At each point, ask how much less of the well than of the controls has been passed so far. That shortfall is the amount of the well still to come, in excess of the controls. Square it, magnify it by how rare that point is among controls, and add it all up.

The same thing as a plain sum. Take every control nanowell whose FC is above the control median, one at a time. At that nanowell's FC, read off F0 and Fn, form the excess gap max(F0 − Fn, 0), multiply it by itself, and divide by 1 − F0. Add those results over all the control nanowells above the median, divide by N, the total number of control nanowells, and multiply by n. That is A². (Here max(a, 0) means "a if a is positive, otherwise 0".)

Why each piece is there. The gap is squared so that large gaps count more than small ones. Only an excess counts, through max(·, 0), so a well whose tail is thinner than the controls' scores 0. Dividing by 1 − F0, the share of controls still above that FC, magnifies gaps far out in the tail: a gap of 0.01 where only 2 % of controls remain counts as much as a gap of 0.05 at the median. Summing over the control nanowells themselves, as the plain-sum form does, gives every control percentile equal weight before that magnification.

A well whose curve lies on the controls' curve. Every gap is 0 and A² = 0.

p-value. The same relabelling of whole wells as HC, using the mean A² of the wells labelled "treated", with the same floor of 1 / C(c + t, t).

4.4 Pooled shape test DESCRIPTIVE

The Anderson–Darling test is a standard test of whether two sets of values come from the same distribution. It compares their cumulative curves along their whole length and responds to a difference in either direction (it is two-sided). Here it is applied to all treated nanowells against all control nanowells, with the wells ignored. Its textbook p-value assumes every nanowell is an independent observation, which is r = 0. Section 3 showed what that assumption does: at r = 0.01, for example, it makes the yardstick 11 times too small in variance. So when wells differ this p-value is far too small. It describes how different the pooled distributions look. It is never used to conclude that a treatment has an effect.

4.6 Floors: the smallest p-value a design can produce

A relabelling p-value is a count divided by the number of labellings, so it cannot be smaller than 1 / C(c + t, t). The floor is fixed by the number of wells and is known before the experiment is run. Figure 18 plots it for 3 treated wells. With 3 control wells the floor is exactly 0.05, so Higher Criticism and the shape test can never fall below 0.05 in a 3 + 3 design, whatever the data. Each added control well lowers the floor. The example plate, with 4 control and 4 treated wells, has 70 labellings and a floor of 1/70 = 0.014.

A second floor applies to a single well. A per-well p-value asks whether one treated well is unusual, by ranking its value of any one statistic (its exceedance rate, its HC or its A²) among the control wells' values of the same statistic:

\[p_{\text{well}} \;=\; \frac{1 + \#\{\text{control wells with a value} \ge \text{this well's}\}}{c + 1} \tag{28}\]

Its floor is 1 / (c + 1): 0.25 with 3 control wells. A group p-value, one per treatment from all its wells together, is what the exceedance model and the relabelling tests give.

Key points of section 4
  • The exceedance test models each well's rate as a draw from a group distribution, with one mean per group on the logit scale and one shared r. It compares the fit with and without a treatment effect.
  • Its result is two numbers: an odds ratio (how large the excess is) and a one-sided p-value (how often chance alone would give an excess this large).
  • Higher Criticism scans every threshold and reports the largest excess. It is strongest when a few nanowells are very extreme.
  • The shape test adds up the excess over the whole upper half. It also responds to a broad shift, so a shape result alone does not mean rare responders.
  • Both supporting tests get their p-values by relabelling whole wells, which cannot give a p-value below 1 / C(c + t, t).
  • The pooled test ignores wells. It is reported and never used to conclude.

5. Testing several treatments: the Benjamini–Hochberg correction

Where we areSection 4 produced one p-value for one treatment against the controls. A real plate carries several treatments, each tested the same way. This section deals with what happens to the meaning of "p below 0.05" when it is asked many times at once.

Why a correction is needed. A test run at the 0.05 level gives a false positive, a result called significant when nothing is there, 5 % of the time when the treatment does nothing. Test 20 ineffective treatments and one false "hit" is expected. When only a few of the treatments tested are real, the false hits can outnumber the real ones: among 20 treatments of which 1 is real, about half of the "hits" at p below 0.05 are false.

What is controlled. The Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) procedure controls the false discovery rate: among the treatments called significant, the expected share that are false is held at 5 %. The guarantee holds when the tests are independent or tend to move together. They move together here: every treatment is compared with the same control wells, so an unusually low set of controls lowers every p-value at once. The guarantee also assumes that each p-value is itself accurate, which section 7 examines. It is less strict than requiring every p < 0.05 / m (the Bonferroni rule), and so loses less power when several treatments are real.

Procedure. Let m be the number of treatments, each tested against the control.

  1. Sort the m p-values from smallest to largest. A p-value's rank is its place in that list: 1 for the smallest.
  2. Give each its own threshold, 0.05 × rank / m. The threshold rises with rank.
  3. Find the largest rank whose p-value is below its threshold. That treatment and every treatment ranked before it are significant, including any whose own p-value is not below its own threshold.

The same result is usually reported as a corrected value q for each treatment:

\[q_{(i)} \;=\; \min_{j \,\ge\, i}\; \frac{m\,p_{(j)}}{j} \tag{29}\]

Here p(j) is the p-value of rank j, and q(i) is the corrected value for the treatment of rank i. In words: multiply each p-value by m / rank; then, if any larger p-value has a smaller product, use that smaller product. The second part keeps q from decreasing as p increases. A treatment is significant when q is strictly below 0.05, which picks out exactly the treatments that step 3 picks out. q is never smaller than p.

Check the extremes

One treatment (m = 1). The threshold is 0.05 × 1 / 1 = 0.05 and q = p. Nothing changes, as it should when only one question is asked.

Many treatments, only one real. If the real one has the smallest p-value, as a strong effect usually will, it has rank 1 and must beat 0.05 / m, the strictest threshold. This is the same as the Bonferroni rule.

Many treatments, all real and all with tiny p-values. The largest of them must beat 0.05 × m / m = 0.05. Nothing is lost, because when everything called significant is real there are no false discoveries to guard against.

Common mistake: judging each p-value against its own threshold alone

The rule is not "a treatment is significant if its p is below its own threshold". It is "find the largest rank that passes; everything up to it is significant". The two can differ. Take three treatments with p = 0.020, 0.021 and 0.022. Their thresholds are 0.017, 0.033 and 0.050. The first fails its own threshold, but the third passes, so all three are significant. Their q-values agree: p × 3 / rank is 0.060, 0.032 and 0.022, and lowering each to the smallest value at any larger p makes all three 0.022.

What this means for each test. Every treatment added makes a hit need a smaller p. The exceedance test has no floor, so there is no cap on the number of treatments: a strong enough effect can always reach the threshold, though each added treatment raises the effect required. Higher Criticism and the shape test do have a floor, and since q is never below p, a design whose floor is 0.05 or more can never yield a q strictly below 0.05, and so never a significant result, from them. A lone hit sitting on the floor survives correction only while floor × m < 0.05: with 3 treated wells, that is 1 treatment at 4 control wells, 4 at 6, and 8 at 8.

Key points of section 5
  • Testing m treatments at 0.05 each produces about 0.05 × m false positives when none is real.
  • The Benjamini–Hochberg procedure holds the expected share of false results among those called significant at 5 %.
  • Sort the p-values, compare each with 0.05 × rank / m, and take everything up to the largest rank that passes.
  • q is the corrected p-value: significant when q is below 0.05, and never smaller than p.
  • A test with a floor, such as the two relabelling tests, can be shut out entirely: if the floor is 0.05, q can never fall below 0.05.

6. What it takes to conclude that a treatment has rare responders

The conclusion rests on the exceedance test alone, and requires all four of:

  1. At least 3 control wells and 2 treated wells, the minimum for the exceedance model to estimate r and give a group p-value.
  2. At least one exceedance among the wells being compared. This can fail only in unusual data, for instance when many nanowells share exactly the highest fold change, so that none lies strictly above the cutoff. With no exceedances there is nothing to count.
  3. q < 0.05 after the Benjamini–Hochberg correction across all m treatments tested on the donor (section 5).
  4. With 16 wells or fewer in the comparison, the relabelling q < 0.05 as well. With so few wells the exceedance p-value is an approximation that comes out too small (about 8 % of no-effect plates pass p < 0.05 with 4 + 4 wells, section 4.1). Relabelling whole wells (section 2.7) gives a p-value that is exact whatever the model gets wrong; corrected the same way, it must agree. Its floor, 1 / C(c + t, t) (section 4.6), then limits the design: 3 + 2 wells, with 10 relabellings and a floor of 0.1, can never give a conclusion, and 4 + 4 wells, with 70, allow a lone hit among up to three treatments (1/70 × 3 = 0.043). When the relabelling p is already at its floor and still does not pass, the result is that the wells cannot decide, not that there are no responders.

The check in the second row. Because the exceedance p-value is an approximation, its false-positive rate should be measured on the experiment's own control wells. The control wells are split into a mock "treated" group and a mock "control" group and tested; no treatment separates them, so every detection is a false positive. The split is repeated in many different ways, and the share of splits with p below 0.05 is the measured false-positive rate. Since the exceedance test needs at least 3 control and 2 treated wells, the mock groups must be at least 3 and 2, which takes 5 control wells. With 5 there are only 10 distinct splits, so the measurement is coarse; more control wells sharpen it. Adding artificial responders to the mock treated wells measures power, the probability of detecting an effect of that size, in the same way.

Three results matter for planning.

  • The false-positive rate is a little above the 5 % the test is meant to hold. At 0 responders the six curves start between 4.7 % and 7.4 %; the highest is the 3 + 2 design with strongly differing wells. Each figure is uncertain by one or two points, but five of the six are above 5 % and their average is about 6 %, so the excess is small and systematic. These designs count each control well against the other control wells; with one cutoff for every well, as in the worked example of section 4.1, the rate is about 8 % (Figure 15). It is weaker for very small p-values when wells differ strongly. A separate simulation used plates of ten treatments, of which six did nothing, two added 10 responders per well and two had a strong effect. On the treatments that did nothing, p below 0.001 occurred three to four times as often as it should. As a result, of all the treatments called significant after the Benjamini–Hochberg correction, 11 % to 15 % were ones that did nothing, where the correction is meant to hold that share at 5 %.
  • When wells differ little (panel A), 10 responders per treated well are detected in 65 % of experiments with 3 + 2 wells, 84 % with 4 + 3 and 93 % with 6 + 3. Fifteen responders are detected in 91 % or more with any of the three designs.
  • When wells differ strongly (panel B), the same 10 responders are detected in only 28 % to 48 % of experiments. Replicates matter most here: 20 responders are detected in 68 % of experiments with 3 + 2 wells but 98 % with 6 + 3. The spread between wells, not the number of nanowells, limits what can be detected, so reducing that spread in the laboratory (placing the same number of cells in every well, handling every well the same way) raises power as much as adding wells does, and more when the effect is small: between panel B and panel A, detection of 10 responders rises by about 40 percentage points for every design, while going from 3 + 2 to 6 + 3 wells within either panel adds 20 to 28. For 20 responders, adding wells helps as much as reducing the spread.

Spend extra wells on the controls. One control well serves every treatment's comparison, while a treated well serves only its own. The control wells also carry most of the estimate of r, and adding them lowers both floors and makes the false-positive check possible. Extra nanowells per well cannot substitute for wells, because they shrink only the within-well part of the variance (section 3.5).

Chapter summary

  1. A rare effect lives in the tail. Ten responders among 1,000 nanowells move the mean by a few percent and the cumulative curve by at most 0.01, so tests of the average miss them. Counting nanowells above a high cutoff, the controls' 99th percentile, looks where the effect is.
  2. The count in a well, K, varies for two reasons. Counting noise is present even when a well's exceedance probability is fixed; for 1,000 nanowells at 1 % its variance is 9.9. Differences between wells add a second term, 9.9 × (n − 1) × r. The two terms add (equation (18)).
  3. r is one number with two readings. Inside a well it is the correlation between two nanowells. Across wells it sets how much wells' own exceedance rates differ. A small r matters because it is multiplied by n − 1: at r = 0.01 the SD of K rises from 3.1 to 10.4.
  4. The replicate is the well. One treated well with a high count proves little, because a single well's chance nudge can produce it. Several treated wells that are all high are convincing, because independent wells are not all nudged the same way. More nanowells per well shrink only the counting noise; only more wells shrink the rest.
  5. r must be estimated, and with few wells it is estimated poorly. It comes from how far replicate wells of the same group disagree beyond counting noise. Four wells give three pieces of information about that, and the estimate tends to come out too small.
  6. The exceedance test is the primary test. It models each well's rate as a draw from a beta distribution, with one mean per group on the logit scale and one shared r. It fits the model with and without a treatment effect, and converts the gain in log-likelihood into a z and a one-sided p-value. Its effect size is the odds ratio.
  7. Two supporting tests describe the kind of difference. Higher Criticism finds an excess of extreme nanowells without fixing a cutoff in advance. The shape test finds any excess in the upper half. Both get their p-values by relabelling whole wells, which cannot give a p-value below 1 / C(c + t, t). A fourth, pooled test ignores wells and is descriptive only.
  8. Testing several treatments needs a correction. The Benjamini–Hochberg procedure holds the expected share of false results among those called significant at 5 %. Each added treatment makes a result need a smaller p-value.
  9. A conclusion needs at least 3 control and 2 treated wells. Five control wells allow the false-positive rate to be checked on the experiment's own controls, and six or more let the supporting tests reach significance when several treatments are tested. Extra wells are best spent on controls, which serve every comparison.
  10. In simulation, the exceedance test holds its false-positive rate near 5 % at the 0.05 level. Its very small p-values are too optimistic when wells differ strongly. Detection depends far more on how much wells differ than on anything in the analysis.

Glossary

Benjamini–Hochberg procedure
A correction for testing several treatments that holds the false discovery rate at a chosen level. Section 5.
Beta distribution
A family of curves for quantities between 0 and 1. Used here for how wells' own exceedance rates are spread. Sections 2.3, 3.2 and 4.1.
Beta-binomial distribution
The distribution of a count when each well first draws its own rate from a beta distribution and then counts with that rate. Sections 2.3 and 3.3.
Binomial distribution
The distribution of a count of independent yes-or-no outcomes that share one probability. Section 2.1.
Bulk
The typical nanowells of a well, as opposed to its extreme ones. Section 1.
Control well
A well that receives no treatment. Section 1.
Correlation
A unit-free measure, between −1 and +1, of how much two random variables move together. Section 2.2.
Counting noise
The scatter in a count that remains when the underlying probability is fixed. Sections 2.1 and 3.2.
Covariance
The average product of two random variables' distances from their means. Section 2.2.
Cumulative curve
For each value, the share of a set of values lying at or below it.
Cutoff
The 99th percentile of fold change among the pooled control nanowells. Section 1.
Baseline (α)
The control group's level on the log-odds scale. Estimated in every fit; it is not the "no effect" fit. Section 4.1.
Effect size
How large a difference is, as opposed to how sure one is that it is real. Here, the odds ratio. Section 4.1.
Effective number of items
The number of independent items that would carry the same information as the correlated items of one group: n / [1 + (n − 1)r]. It can never exceed 1 / r. Section 2.3.
Exceedance
A nanowell whose fold change is above the cutoff. Section 1.
Exceedance rate
A well's exceedance count divided by its number of nanowells, K / n: what is observed. Section 3.5. Distinct from the well's underlying rate πi, which is never observed. Section 4.1.
False discovery rate
Among the results called significant, the expected share that are false. Section 5.
False positive
A result called significant when the treatment truly does nothing.
Floor
The smallest p-value a design can produce, fixed by its number of wells. Sections 2.7 and 4.6.
Fold change (FC)
Cells at the end divided by cells at the start, for one nanowell. Section 1.
Gap (δ)
The treated group's log-odds minus the control group's: the treatment effect, and the logarithm of the odds ratio. Positive when treated wells are higher. Section 4.1.
Higher Criticism (HC)
A statistic that finds an excess of extreme values at whichever threshold shows it best. Section 4.2.
Intra-well correlation (r)
The correlation between two nanowells of the same well; equivalently, the size of the differences between wells' own rates. Section 3.2.
Likelihood, log-likelihood
The probability a model gives to the observed data, read as a function of the model's parameters, and its logarithm. Section 2.6.
Likelihood-ratio statistic (D)
Twice the gain in log-likelihood from freeing one parameter of a model; used to test whether that parameter is needed. Never negative, and without a direction. Section 2.6.
Logit
The natural logarithm of the odds. Section 2.4.
Monte Carlo method
Any method that reaches its answer by generating many random or rearranged data sets. Section 2.7.
Model, parameter
A mathematical description of how data could have arisen, and the unknown numbers it contains. Section 2.6.
Maximum-likelihood estimate
The parameter values under which the observed data were most probable. Section 2.6.
Nanowell
One of about a thousand small chambers of cells inside a well. Section 1.
Null hypothesis
The supposition that the treatment does nothing, which a test starts from. Section 2.7.
Null distribution
The values a test statistic takes when the treatment does nothing. Section 2.7.
Odds, odds ratio
A probability divided by one minus itself; and the factor by which a treatment multiplies the odds. Sections 2.4 and 4.1.
One-sided test
A test in which only a departure in one direction counts as evidence.
p-value
The probability, if the null hypothesis were true, of a result at least as extreme as the one observed.
Overdispersion
More variation in a count than the binomial distribution allows. Section 2.3.
Plate
A plastic tray with a grid of separate wells, commonly 96. Section 1.
Percentile
The value that a stated percentage of a set of values lie at or below.
Power
The probability that a test detects an effect of a given size.
q-value
A p-value after the Benjamini–Hochberg correction, also called the adjusted p-value. Section 5.
Relabelling test
A test that finds its p-value by trying every way of assigning the labels "treated" and "control" to the wells; also called a permutation test. Sections 2.7 and 4.2.
Replicate
One independent repetition of a treatment. Here, a well. Section 1.
Responder
A nanowell that the treatment made grow far more than untreated nanowells do. A cause, not an observation: compare Exceedance. Section 1.
Simulation
Measuring a method's behaviour by applying it to many artificial data sets in which the truth is known. Section 2.7.
Standard deviation (SD)
The square root of the variance; a typical distance from the mean.
Two-sided test
A test that responds to a difference in either direction. Section 4.
Upper tail
The highest values of a distribution. Section 1.
Variance
The average squared distance from the mean.
Well
One compartment of a plate, holding about 1,000 nanowells and receiving one treatment. Section 1.
