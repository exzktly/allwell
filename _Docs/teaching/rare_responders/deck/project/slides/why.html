Imaging counts every cell, every day: better than a sample. Yet the 64 responder cells are still 0.1 % of the well.

The problem, in a standard 96-well proliferation study. Each well starts with about fifty thousand T cells and dendritic cells, and only a handful of T cells, here two, recognise the test molecule. By day 4 they have divided five times into 64 cells, while cytokines such as IL-2 that they release spread through the shared medium and push bystander cells to divide too: the treated well holds 63,500 cells. Start with flow cytometry. It reads a sample of the well, here 20,000 of its 63,500 cells, so only about 20 of the 64 responder cells are acquired. In the dye-dilution plot they are a bump that shows only when the plot is zoomed a hundredfold, far from one broad main peak; the bystanders the cytokines nudged only widen that peak a little, and the control well has its own spread. It also needs a proliferation dye or a reporter, gives one snapshot rather than a time course, and cuts each cell off from the dendritic cell that stimulated it. Imaging the whole well does a little better: every cell is counted, all 64 responder cells included, and the well can be followed day by day without a dye. But the responders are still 0.1 % of the well, and the bystander lift lands in the same total, so the treated well ends only 4 % above the control, inside the ordinary well-to-well spread.
