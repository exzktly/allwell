The right panel separates two questions the model keeps apart. δ asks whether one group is higher than the other. r asks whether wells of the same group disagree with one another by more than counting noise. The four small plots show every combination: a gap with wells that agree, which is molecule A; a gap with one wild well; no gap with wells that agree; and no gap with one wild well, which is close to molecule B. A gap does not put anything into r, because r is measured around each group’s own average. Both numbers are fitted in step 3 and in step 4; r is never fixed. This slide explains a number that could look like a contradiction. Slide 12 said the control and A wells scatter about as much as counting noise alone gives, so r is about zero; yet step 4 reports r = 0.0027 for the same eight wells. The top row is step 3: each group has its own mean, and the shaded bands, where 95 percent of wells should fall under counting noise alone, comfortably hold every well. The middle row forces all eight wells to share one mean, 17, while keeping r at zero. The band is just as narrow, but now the control wells sit at or beyond its lower edge and the A wells at or beyond its upper edge; under that fit the counts are very improbable, log L = -32.3. The only way the one-group model can account for wells this far from their shared mean is to say that wells differ a lot from one another, that is, to raise r. The bottom row is the actual step 4: r is refitted to 0.0027, the band widens to cover the wells, and log L recovers to −27.4. This matters for the test. Had r been held at zero, step 3 would beat the one-group fit by 12.1 in log L; with r refitted it is 7.12. By letting scatter between wells explain part of the difference, the test becomes more cautious about calling a gap real. That is intended, because wells genuinely do differ.
