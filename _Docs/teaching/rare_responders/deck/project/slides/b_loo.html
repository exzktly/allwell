A possible refinement of step 1. The test counts every well, control or molecule, against one pooled cutoff, the 99th percentile of all 4,000 control nanowells, so exactly 40 of those nanowells pass it. A molecule well is compared with a cutoff it had no part in setting, but a control well helps set its own. The refinement would treat control wells as molecule wells are treated: each control well counted against a cutoff from the other three control wells only. On this plate’s nanowells it would change the control counts from 7, 8, 14 and 11 to 7, 8, 14 and 13. The simulation shows why. On the left the cutoff is set from all four control wells, each well included in its own cutoff. A well whose fold changes run slightly high pulls the pooled cutoff up, which hides part of its own excess, so its count is drawn back toward 10. The control wells then look more alike than they really are: the variance of their counts is about 17. On the right each well is judged by the other three, and the variance is about 36. Neither is the true figure: with a cutoff known in advance the variance would be about 25. The shared cutoff understates it and leave-one-out overstates it, so leave-one-out errs on the cautious side. This matters because r is estimated from how much wells of the same group differ, control wells included. If they are made to look too similar, r comes out too small, and a scatter that is too small makes every p-value too small. The simulated wells differ slightly in their average fold change, as real wells do.
