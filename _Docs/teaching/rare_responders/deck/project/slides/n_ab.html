Molecules A and B make the role of r concrete. Both have an average count of 24 against the control’s 10, so judged by averages alone they look the same. On the left, A’s four wells are all close to 24 and the control wells close to 10. Neither group scatters beyond what counting noise alone gives, so r is about zero, the bands of expected counts are narrow and overlap only between about 14 and 16, with every A well above every control well, and the gap between the groups cannot be put down to chance: z = 3.77, p = 0.00008. On the right, B’s average of 24 comes from one well at 64 and three near 10. That is far more scatter than counting noise, so the fit sets r to 0.008. With that much scatter between wells, the range of counts expected by chance is several times wider, the two groups’ ranges overlap almost completely, and a gap of this size is unsurprising: z = 0.95, p = 0.17. The fitted averages are 13.3 for control and 20.8 for B rather than the plain 10 and 24: with this much scatter the model’s curve has a long right tail, so its average sits above where most wells fall, and the single well at 64 pulls less than it does in a plain average. So r is never the thing being tested, but it decides the test: the more wells scatter, the larger a gap chance alone can produce. One well with many responders is not evidence; four wells that agree are.
