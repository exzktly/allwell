Simulated end to end, from nanowells through the pooled cutoff (4 + 4, 40,000 plates): 8.1 % pass p < 0.05, 2.2 % pass 0.01, 0.33 % pass 0.001

The p-value of this test rests on a large-sample approximation, so it is fair to ask how well it holds with four and four wells. We simulated plates in which the molecule does nothing, with 1,000 nanowells per well, a rate of 1.7 percent and r = 0.0027, the fit without a gap for A, and counted how often the test gave p below 0.05. It should be 5 percent. With four control and four molecule wells it was about 7.4 percent, so the p-value is somewhat too small. The reason is that r, the well-to-well scatter, is estimated from very few wells and usually comes out too small. Adding wells helps only slowly: with eight and eight wells it was still about 6.6 percent. The same holds when the plates are simulated from the nanowells up, with the cutoff estimated from each plate’s own control wells: 8.1 percent of no-effect plates passed p below 0.05, 2.2 percent passed 0.01 and 0.33 percent passed 0.001, so the smaller the p-value, the more it overstates the evidence: about 1.6, 2.2 and 3.2 times the promised rate. For this plate the practical reading is simple. In the simulations, z had to exceed about 1.91, not 1.64, to keep false calls at 5 percent. A’s z of 3.77 is far beyond that. C’s z of 1.74 is below it, so C should be treated as not shown, which is also what the correction across molecules concluded. B is not close. In general, trust a p-value far below 0.05 and be wary of one just below it; the shuffling tests of slide 25 do not rely on this approximation.
