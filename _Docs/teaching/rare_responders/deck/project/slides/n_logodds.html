δ above 0: A is higher than control, and below 0: lower

The model’s two rates are not fitted directly; they are written on the log-odds scale, and this slide shows that this is only a change of scale. Take the control: on average 10 of 1,000 nanowells pass, a rate of 1.0 percent. Its odds are the rate divided by one minus the rate, 0.0101, about 1 to 99, and the natural log of that is −4.60. That number is called α. Molecule A: 24 of 1,000, 2.4 percent, odds 0.0246, log-odds −3.71. The difference between the two log-odds, 0.89, is δ, the gap. So the model line reads: the log-odds of well i’s rate equals α plus δ times x for well i, where that x is 0 for a control well and 1 for a well given the molecule, and π for well i is simply the rate of the group the well belongs to. Why this scale? Because “the molecule does nothing” becomes the single statement δ = 0, whatever the control rate happens to be, and because any values of α and δ correspond to valid rates between 0 and 1, so the fitting can move them freely. A difference of log-odds is a ratio of odds: e to the 0.89 is 2.43, so A’s odds of a nanowell passing are about 2.4 times the control’s. The test will ask whether the data are compatible with δ = 0.
