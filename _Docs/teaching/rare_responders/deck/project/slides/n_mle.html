With a score in hand, fitting is a search: find the guess of α, δ and r with the highest log-likelihood. That is all maximum likelihood means. The left panel shows the search along the number we care about, the gap δ. Each point of the curve is the best log L that the other two numbers, α and r, can reach at that value of δ; plotted against δ this is a hill. Its top is at δ = 0.89, where log L is −20.2. The computer does not try every value; it starts from a guess, computes log L, nudges the three numbers in the direction that raises it, and stops when no nudge helps. For A against control the search returns α = −4.60, a control rate of 1.0 percent, and α + δ = −3.71, an A rate of 2.4 percent. These are just the group averages, 10 and 24 of 1,000: when r is near zero, the best rate for a group is its average count over n. And r comes out near zero because neither group’s counts vary beyond what counting noise gives: the control counts have a variance of 10.0 against 9.9 expected from counting alone, about what counting noise alone gives, and A’s 8.7 against 23. Step 4 of the algorithm is a second, separate search with one restriction: δ is held at zero, so all eight wells share one rate. Its result is the open circle at the left end of the hill, log L = −27.4, and there r is no longer zero but 0.0027; slide 17 explains why. The difference in height between the two results, 7.12, is what the test will use.
