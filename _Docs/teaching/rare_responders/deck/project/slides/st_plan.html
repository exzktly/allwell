Three conditions together justify calling a molecule a source of rare responders. There must be at least three control wells and two treated wells, because with fewer the model cannot estimate how much wells differ, and without that a high well cannot be told apart from chance. There must be at least one nanowell past the cutoff among the wells compared. And the molecule’s q value, after the Benjamini–Hochberg correction across every molecule tested, must be below 0.05. For planning, five control wells allow a useful self-check: split the control wells into a mock treated group of two and a mock control group of three, run the test, and repeat for every split; since nothing separates them, every call is a false positive, and the share of calls measures the false-positive rate on the experiment’s own wells. Six or more control wells with three treated also let the supporting tests, which get their p-values by shuffling well labels, can reach smaller p-values. The six-donor plate gives each donor four control and four treated wells per molecule: enough for a conclusion, one control well short of the self-check. Groups are defined per donor, so each donor’s molecule wells are compared with that donor’s own control wells, because T-cell responses differ between people; the model here has no term for the donor or for the plate. If no control nanowell passes the cutoff, r cannot be estimated and the p-value overstates the evidence. With four control wells r is estimated only roughly and usually too small, which makes the p-value too small; more control wells give a better estimate.
