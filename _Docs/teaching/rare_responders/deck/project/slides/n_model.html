Before any fitting, this slide states the model in words and in a picture. A group of replicate wells, the control wells or the wells given molecule A, has one underlying rate, π: the chance that a nanowell passes the cutoff, the same π as on slide 10. Wells are never identical, so each well gets its own rate, πᵢ, scattered around the group’s π; the single number r says how wide that scatter is. Finally the well’s count K is what we observe: each of its thousand nanowells passes with chance πᵢ, and that step adds counting noise. πᵢ itself is never estimated; the beta-binomial of slide 10 is this two-stage story with πᵢ averaged out. The right panel shows what the two kinds of unknown look like in data. π sets where the group’s wells sit on average, the bar. r sets how far wells may stray from it: the shaded band is where counting noise alone would put 95 percent of wells. All control and A wells are inside their bands, so their data ask for no extra scatter and r comes out near zero. B has the same average as A, 24, but one well at 64 and three near 10, far more scatter than counting noise, so B’s test needs r = 0.008. The dashed box is the same kind of range as the shaded band, redrawn for that r: where 95 percent of B’s wells should fall when wells differ that much. It is several times taller than the shaded band, and the well at 64 is still above it. When A is tested against control there are three unknowns: π for control, π for A, and one r shared by both; with four wells per group there is too little information to estimate a separate scatter for each. The values of r quoted here are results of the fit that the next slides build up.
