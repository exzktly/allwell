Now the test itself. The two fits gave log-likelihoods of -20.25 with a gap and -27.37 without, a difference of 7.12. The likelihood-ratio statistic, D, is twice that difference, 14.24; it is called a ratio because a difference of logs is the log of a ratio of the two likelihoods. The larger it is, the more the gap helps to explain the counts. But how large is large? A fit with a gap can never score lower than one without, because setting the gap to zero is one of its options; so a higher log-likelihood alone proves nothing. Panel 1 is a plot with two axes. Up the left is the gain in log-likelihood over the fit without a gap: 0 is the one-group level, which runs along the x axis, and 7.12 is A’s two-group level at the top. Across the bottom is the number of plates. Each orange bar is a sideways histogram bin: its height on the left axis is a gain, 0 to 0.5, 0.5 to 1 and so on, and its length is how many of 1,500 simulated plates in which the molecule does nothing gained that much: in 1,500 simulated no-effect plates the gain is usually a few tenths, 95 percent of them gain less than 2.6, and none gained as much as A’s 7.12, in either direction. The test asks whether A’s gain is more than that. Giving a model an extra free number always improves its fit a little, even when it is not needed. Statistical theory says how much: if the molecule truly did nothing, D would behave like the square of z, where z is a value drawn from the standard normal distribution, mean 0 and standard deviation 1; that is the chi-square distribution with one degree of freedom; the factor of two is what makes that hold. So under chance alone D is usually below about 4. To use this, take the square root of D and give it the sign of δ: z = +3.77, positive because A sits above the control. The curve on the right is what z would look like if A did nothing; 3.77 is far out in its tail. The test is one-sided, because only a rise counts as a response, so p is the area of the curve beyond z: 0.00008. The grey bars are a check on that theory: z values from 1,500 simulated plates of four and four wells in which the molecule does nothing, with the scatter of step 4. They follow the curve, but a little wider: across this and a second batch of simulations about 8 percent pass z = 1.64 where the curve says 5 percent, and the largest of these 1,500 was 3.3, the most extreme in either direction −3.7. So with four and four wells p is approximate and somewhat too small; A’s z of 3.77 is still beyond every simulated no-effect plate.
