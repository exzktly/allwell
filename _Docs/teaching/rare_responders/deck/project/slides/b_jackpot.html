Molecule B deserves a careful reading, because “not detected” is not the same as “does nothing”. One of B’s four wells has 64 nanowells past the cutoff; the other three look like control wells. There are two readings. In the first, the well is an accident: something about its seeding, its dendritic cells, its position on the plate or its imaging, which would have happened whatever molecule was in it. In the second, the well holds a real response: a rare T cell that recognises B happened to land in that well and not in the others, and it expanded. If responding T cells are rare enough, most wells would contain none, and one well in four lighting up is exactly what a real response would look like. The test cannot tell these apart. It asks whether B raises the rate across wells, and with one high well among four the honest answer is that there is no evidence for that. Both readings produce the same four counts. What would separate them is more data: repeating B with more wells, where a real but rare response predicts further high wells at a low rate; looking inside the well to check that the 64 nanowells are genuine expansions; and checking whether the same well position stands out for other molecules. If responses are expected to be that rare, the better design counts responding wells rather than nanowells.
