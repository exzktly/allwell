The previous slide ends on a question: if a few responders are lost among fifty thousand cells in one shared well, how could we see their proliferation directly? Divide the well. On the left is the bulk well from the previous slide at day 4: the two responders, ringed, have grown into small clones, but they are a few dozen cells in a crowd, and the cytokines they release spread through the shared medium and lift their neighbours too. In the middle the same footprint is divided into many small wells, called nanowells; a real well holds about a thousand of them. Cells settle into them at random, so each nanowell holds one dendritic cell and a few T cells: its own small co-culture. Now a responder is one of a few cells in its nanowell rather than one of fifty thousand, so when it divides its nanowell visibly fills, and the walls keep its cytokines with it, so its neighbours are not lifted. On the right are nine nanowells close up: the outlined one has filled, the others still hold one to three T cells. Because the cells are held in place, plain images taken over time are enough to count them, with no dye and no reporter line.
